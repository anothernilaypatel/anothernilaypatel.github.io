Regulatory Entities and Market Participants

The SEC

  • The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) is a federal agency, created by the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 after the 1929 crash. It is not an SRO.
  • Mission: protect investors; maintain fair, orderly, and efficient markets; facilitate capital formation.
  • 5 commissioners, appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate, staggered 5-year terms. No more than 3 from the same party. The President names the Chairman.
  • Terms expire June 5. A commissioner whose term has ended may keep serving until a successor is in place, for no more than 18 months (the carryover).
  • Jurisdiction: securities exchanges, broker-dealers and their reps, investment advisers and mutual funds, public-company disclosure, enforcement of federal securities laws.
  • Outside SEC jurisdiction: insurance (state regulators), bank deposits, commodity futures (CFTC).
  • EDGAR holds issuer filings only (registration statements, 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K), free to the public. A rep’s registration or disciplinary history is BrokerCheck (FINRA), not EDGAR.
  • Enforcement is civil only: lawsuits, injunctions, fines, disgorgement. Criminal penalties go to the Department of Justice.
  • The SEC has ultimate oversight of SROs. Proposed SRO rule changes are filed with the SEC; some need approval before they take effect, and designated categories become effective on filing.
  • A final FINRA disciplinary action can be reviewed by the SEC only after FINRA’s own review (which may include the National Adjudicatory Council). Those appeals are not DOJ criminal cases.

Self-regulatory organizations

  • An SRO is non-governmental. It writes and enforces member rules under authority delegated by the SEC. The SEC, not the Treasury, the Fed, or Congress, is the source of that oversight.
  • Major SROs:

  • FINRA is the largest SRO. Formed in 2007 by merging the NASD and the NYSE’s regulatory functions. The 1934 Act created the SEC, not FINRA.

  • Regulation T initial margin is 50% of the purchase price (deposit at least half). The Fed sets initial margin. FINRA sets ongoing maintenance margin. This chapter does not give the maintenance percentage.
  • Blue-sky laws are state securities laws on top of federal law. The name refers to schemes with nothing behind them but “blue sky.”

SIPC (Securities Investor Protection Act of 1970): a nonprofit membership corporation, not a government agency. Most SEC-registered broker-dealers must be members, with statutory exceptions. SIPC is not an SRO. FINRA writes and enforces conduct rules; SIPC restores missing customer cash and securities after a member fails. Firms must give written SIPC disclosure at account opening and annually.

SIPC limit Amount
Securities + cash, per customer $500,000
Cash sublimit (inside the total, not added on top) $250,000
  • Each separate capacity (individual, joint, IRA) is its own customer with its own $500,000.
  • Cash is tested against $250,000 first, then the combined claim against $500,000.
  • $300,000 securities + $200,000 cash: cash is under the sublimit and the combined $500,000 is at the cap, so the full cash is covered.
  • $400,000 securities + $300,000 cash = $700,000 claimed. Recovery stops at $500,000, and no more than $250,000 of that recovery can be cash. The overall cap binds.
  • SIPC does not cover: market losses, bad advice or unsuitable recommendations, fraud losses (it covers missing assets, not a fraud scheme’s losses), commodity futures, fixed annuities, or assets held outside a broker-dealer.

FDIC: federal agency, funded by bank premiums. Insures checking, savings, CDs, and money-market deposit accounts up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank. Never securities or mutual funds. SIPC is funded by broker-dealer assessments.

Investment advisers:

  • Paid for investment advice. Fiduciary duty: client’s best interest.
  • Broker-dealers recommending to retail customers follow Regulation Best Interest (Reg BI) — best interest, not the full adviser fiduciary duty.
  • AUM is the adviser firm’s assets, not one representative’s book. An IAR does not SEC-register off the firm’s AUM.
  • General AUM rule: state registration below $100 million; may register with the SEC from $100 million up to but not including $110 million; must register with the SEC at $110 million or more, unless exempt.
  • An adviser already SEC-registered may stay while AUM is $90 million or more (buffer against flipping).
  • An adviser to a registered investment company registers with the SEC at any AUM. Other special rules can apply below the usual thresholds.

Other roles:

  • Issuer: creates and sells securities to raise capital (corporation, municipality, government).
  • Underwriter (investment bank): buys from the issuer and resells to the public. Must disclose its participation or interest in a primary or secondary distribution. May not take payment from the issuer, or the issuer’s affiliates or promoters, to publish a quote or make a market. Ordinary underwriting fees are allowed.
  • Municipal advisor: advises state and local governments on bond issues and financial products. MSRB writes the rules; the SEC enforces them. Owes a fiduciary duty to the municipal client. Underwriters deal with the issuer at arm’s length.
  • Market maker: dealer that always quotes a bid and an ask (two-sided market) and earns the spread.
  • Custodian: holds and safeguards client assets. Trustee: fiduciary duty to the beneficiary.
  • Transfer agent: ownership records, transfers on the issuer’s books, issues and cancels certificates, pays dividends. Does not settle trades (that is the DTCC). Regulated by the SEC.
  • Registrar (offerings unit): checks that shares issued do not exceed the charter.

  • SEC = government agency, civil cases only. FINRA and the MSRB = SROs. Criminal cases = DOJ. SIPC ≠ government and ≠ SRO.
  • MSRB never enforces: broker-dealers → FINRA; municipal advisors → SEC; bank dealers → federal banking regulator.
  • EDGAR = issuer filings (10-K, prospectus). BrokerCheck = person or firm registration and discipline.
  • Fed = initial margin (50%, Reg T). FINRA = maintenance margin.
  • FDIC = bank deposits, $250,000. SIPC = failed broker-dealer, $500,000 with $250,000 cash inside that cap. SIPC ignores a market decline.
  • NASAA coordinates. The state regulator enforces blue-sky laws.
  • Home equity is excluded from the $1 million accredited net-worth test.
  • Introducing broker does not custody. Clearing broker does.
  • Adviser = fiduciary. Retail broker-dealer recommendation = Reg BI.
  • SEC registration thresholds ($100 million / $110 million / $90 million buffer) are measured at the adviser, not the representative.
  • OCC guarantees options. DTCC clears equities, bonds, and mutual funds.
  • FINRA’s birth year is 2007, not 1934.

Market Structure

Remember. Exchange = listed = auction; OTC = unlisted = negotiated. Listed stock can still trade OTC in the third market.

Primary and secondary are about who gets paid. Third and fourth are both secondary trades. The difference is whether a dealer stands in the middle.

  • The third and fourth markets are secondary-market activity (already-issued securities).
  • A different labeling system splits secondary trading into a first market (listed, on an exchange), a second market (unlisted, OTC), plus third and fourth. All four of those labels are secondary trading. The second market is not the secondary market.

The primary market

  • New securities are created and sold for the first time. The issuer receives the proceeds. Underwriters price, market, and distribute.
  • Primary transactions: IPO (first public sale; private company becomes public), follow-on offering (new shares from an already-public company), municipal new issue.
  • A follow-on (also called an additional primary offering) pays the issuer. A secondary offering pays the selling shareholders; the company receives nothing.
  • A registered secondary offering of already-issued shares stays a secondary-market trade even with a registration, a prospectus, and an underwriter. It is a formal distribution. An ordinary exchange or OTC trade is ongoing secondary trading. Both involve existing shares. Classify by who sells: issuer (new shares) or shareholders (old shares).
  • Corporate buyers get a prospectus. Municipal buyers get an official statement. Underwriters do due diligence on the issuer’s disclosures. The underwriter is paid the spread; the issuer gets the proceeds.

The secondary market

  • Existing securities trade. The issuer is paid nothing. Volume dwarfs the primary market.
  • Two jobs: liquidity (convert to cash without waiting for maturity or an issuer buyback) and price discovery (trading sets current value).

The third market

  • Exchange-listed securities traded OTC, off the exchange. The listing does not change; the venue does. Non-exchange member firms can trade the listed stock without the exchange.
  • Used by institutions and dealers. A dealer is still the intermediary, and the trade is negotiated, not run through the exchange auction.
  • Three reasons: lower cost (no exchange fees or floor-broker commissions), less market impact on a large block, and hours beyond the exchange session.
  • It exists for cost and market-impact reasons, not because the security is ineligible for the exchange.
  • Unlisted OTC trading is the ordinary OTC market, not the third market.

The fourth market

  • Institution-to-institution, no broker-dealer. Done on ECNs, which match buy and sell orders at specified prices for both listed and OTC securities.
  • Used by large institutions. Benefits: no commissions or markups, anonymity (a block does not advertise itself and move the price), and immediate electronic execution.
  • ECNs are not limited to listed securities. The third market is the venue defined as listed securities trading off-exchange.

Key concepts across all markets

  • Liquidity: buy or sell without a large price move. It comes from the secondary market, not the primary. More trading tightens spreads. Listed names are generally more liquid than OTC. Illiquid securities are harder to sell at a fair price.
  • Price discovery: trades reveal what buyers and sellers will pay. Exchanges (more transparent) discover price more efficiently than OTC. Prices are set by trading, not by a regulator or the issuer.
  • Market makers keep a continuous two-sided quote. Profit is the spread. On Nasdaq and OTC, several market makers can compete in the same security and narrow the spread.

Exam traps

  • Primary pays the issuer. Secondary pays the seller. A prospectus plus an underwriter does not turn a shareholder resale into a primary sale.
  • Follow-on / additional primary = new shares, issuer paid. Secondary offering = old shares, shareholder paid.
  • Third = listed stock, OTC, dealer present. Fourth = ECN, no broker-dealer. Any OTC trade of an unlisted stock is not the third market.
  • ECNs match listed and OTC orders.
  • “Second market” (unlisted OTC) is not the “secondary market.”
  • Prospectus = corporate. Official statement = municipal.
  • Liquidity and the ability to exit come from the secondary market.
  • Nasdaq is an exchange now; remember the dealer-market origin.
  • Defensive wording elsewhere: “auction = NYSE competitive bidding; negotiated = OTC/Nasdaq dealer quotes.”

Inflation indicators

  • CPI (Bureau of Labor Statistics, monthly): price change in a basket of consumer goods and services. The most cited inflation gauge. What consumers pay.
  • PPI: change in wholesale prices producers receive. A leading indicator of CPI. If PPI rises, CPI often follows.

U.S. balance of payments

Records every economic transaction between the U.S. and the rest of the world.

Account What it records
Current Trade in goods and services, plus income and transfers
Capital Capital transfers (debt forgiveness) and transfers of nonproduced, nonfinancial assets
Financial Investment flows: foreigners buying U.S. assets, and U.S. buyers of foreign assets (including U.S. stocks and bonds)
  • Trade deficit: imports exceed exports. Trade surplus: exports exceed imports. The U.S. has run a trade deficit for decades.
  • The trade balance is only part of the current account. Income and transfers can change the current-account result, so a trade deficit alone does not prove a current-account deficit.
  • When the current and capital accounts together are in deficit, financial-account inflows finance the gap. Foreign purchases of U.S. securities belong in the financial account, not the capital account.

Exchange rates

The price of one currency in another.

What moves the exchange rate:

  • Higher U.S. interest rates attract foreign capital and strengthen the dollar.
  • Higher inflation weakens the currency.
  • A large trade deficit can weaken the dollar over time.
  • Political stability attracts investment and strengthens the currency.

Chains:

  • Fed raises rates → dollar up → imports cheaper, exports costlier → trade deficit may widen.
  • Fed cuts rates → dollar down → imports costlier, exports cheaper → trade deficit may narrow.
  • Strong expansion → consumers import more → deficit grows.
  • Recession → imports fall → deficit shrinks.
  • “Rates” in that chain means interest rates, not the exchange rate. Foreign investors sell home currency, buy dollars, and buy higher-yielding U.S. assets.

Exam traps

  • The Fed does not set the federal-funds rate itself; it sets a target range. It does set the discount rate.
  • Buy securities = ease (rates down, bond prices up, dollar down). Sell = tighten (the reverse).
  • QE and QT are monetary, not fiscal spending.
  • Bond prices and yields never move the same direction.
  • Stock market = leading. Unemployment rate = lagging. Initial claims = leading. Non-farm payrolls = coincident. Publication lag does not change that class.
  • PPI leads CPI. CPI is the consumer basket (BLS, monthly).
  • Defensive stocks = essentials (utilities, food, healthcare), not military contractors. This lesson also calls them counter-cyclical.
  • Keynesian = fiscal. Monetarist = monetary.
  • Balance sheet = a date. Income statement = a period.
  • GDP = inside the border. GNP = by nationality. Recession uses real GDP, two quarters.
  • Trade deficit = imports greater than exports. Foreign buying of U.S. stocks and bonds is the financial account.
  • Strong dollar helps U.S. consumers and hurts U.S. exporters.

Shelf registrations

  • An eligible issuer registers a large amount once and sells pieces for up to 3 years from the initial effective date. Securities sit unsold until the issuer takes them off the shelf. Filing is not the sale date.
  • Each takedown uses a prospectus supplement, not a new full registration.
  • Benefits: sell into a favorable market, avoid a new registration each time, raise capital in portions.
  • Practical users already report to the SEC. A first-time issuer has no reporting history and uses an ordinary registration.
  • Well-Known Seasoned Issuer (WKSI): public float of $700 million or more, or at least $1 billion of non-convertible securities issued in the prior 3 years. A WKSI automatic shelf is effective on filing, with no SEC review wait. Other issuers wait for the SEC to declare the shelf effective.

Regulatory requirements and exemptions

  • The 1933 Act requires registration of securities offered to the public. Review is disclosure-based. The SEC does not grade the investment’s merit.
  • Exempt securities (no federal registration): U.S. government, municipal, bank, and nonprofit securities. Exempt transactions include private placements and certain secondary trades.
  • Exempt from registration is not exempt from anti-fraud rules (federal or state).
  • A safe harbor means: meet every condition and the offering (or resale) is treated as allowed; miss one and the protection drops.

  • Reg T initial margin 50% (Fed). FINRA sets maintenance; no percent in this chapter.
  • Prime rate ≈ federal funds + 3 percentage points. Order: fed funds, then discount, then prime.
  • Reserve requirements 0% since March 26, 2020. Primary credit at the top of the fed-funds range since March 2020.
  • SIPC $500,000 per customer, cash sublimit $250,000 inside that cap. Separate capacities (individual, joint, IRA) each get $500,000. Written SIPC notice at opening and annually.
  • FDIC $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank.
  • Worked SIPC claims: $300,000 securities + $200,000 cash is fully covered; $400,000 + $300,000 = $700,000 claimed and pays $500,000 with cash still capped at $250,000.

People and advisers

  • Accredited: net worth over $1 million excluding the home; income over $200,000 / $300,000 joint in each of the last two years. Series 7, 65, or 82 also qualifies.
  • Adviser AUM: state below $100 million; SEC optional from $100 million to under $110 million; SEC required at $110 million. Stay-registered buffer $90 million. Investment-company advisers: SEC at any AUM.
  • Affiliate = officer, director, or 10%+ shareholder.

Cycle, indicators, currency

  • Cycle order: expansion, peak, contraction, trough. Recession = two consecutive quarters of declining real GDP.
  • GDP first print about 1 month after the quarter, then two more revisions. Payrolls about 3 weeks after the month, then two revisions over two months.

Offerings

  • Registration often effective about 20 days after filing (SEC review can change that). Prospectus at or before the sale.
  • Shelf life 3 years. WKSI: public float $700 million or more, or $1 billion non-convertible securities in 3 years. Automatic effectiveness.
  • Form D within 15 days of the first sale.
  • Rule 504: $10 million in 12 months. Rule 506(b): no general solicitation, up to 35 non-accredited sophisticated buyers. Rule 506(c): accredited only, status verified, solicitation allowed.
  • Rule 144 resale: 6 months (reporting) or 12 months (non-reporting). Affiliate volume, rolling 3 months: greater of 1% of outstanding or average weekly volume for the prior 4 weeks (reporting); 1% only (non-reporting).
  • Form 144 when an affiliate’s sales exceed 5,000 shares or $50,000; file the day the order is placed.
  • Rule 144A: QIB owns and invests at least $100 million. No hold and no volume limit on sales to QIBs.
  • Rule 147: intrastate. Offered and sold only inside one state.
  • Reg A Tier 1: $20 million / 12 months; Form 1-Z within 30 days after the offering ends. Tier 2: $75 million / 12 months; Form 1-K, Form 1-SA, Form 1-U.
  • Issuer registration form for a public deal: Form S-1. Ongoing issuer reports: 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K.

Equity Securities

Why this order. What you own, then the ways to acquire common stock, then the resale limits.

Common Stock

  • Common stock = ownership. Holders are residual owners (paid only after creditors and preferred).
  • Limited liability: maximum loss is the amount invested. Creditors cannot reach personal assets.
  • Return = dividends (if declared) + capital appreciation. Upside is unlimited; downside stops at the investment.
  • Dividends are declared only by the board of directors, paid from after-tax earnings, and are never a right.
  • Cash dividends are taxed as ordinary income or as qualified dividends.
  • Shareholders vote on: board elections, stock splits, mergers and acquisitions, and issuing new shares.
  • A proxy is written authorization for someone else to vote the shares.

  • Example: 200 shares and 4 seats = 800 votes. Statutory: max 200 per seat. Cumulative: all 800 on one candidate.

Exam traps

  • There is no guaranteed dividend. The right is to receive a dividend only if the board declares one.
  • Limited liability still allows loss of the entire investment.
  • Statutory helps the majority; cumulative helps the minority.
  • Do not reverse the dividend-date order. Ex-date is when the stock trades without the dividend.

Preferred Stock

  • Hybrid of equity and debt: ownership plus a fixed dividend (a rate or dollar amount, not a guarantee).
  • Board must still declare each dividend. Skipping preferred is not a default. A missed bond coupon is a default.
  • Paid before common. In liquidation, ahead of common and behind all debt.
  • Generally no voting rights. Price moves inversely with interest rates, like a bond.

  • Callable preferred benefits the issuer. Issuers call when rates fall and reissue at a lower dividend. Holder faces call risk (loss of a high dividend).
  • Convertible and participating preferred carry lower stated dividends because the extra feature has value.

Exam traps

  • A fixed dividend is a stated rate, not a promise. No declaration, no payment, and no default.
  • Callable preferred benefits the issuer, not the investor.
  • Convertible and participating preferred pay less, not more, than straight preferred.

Rights

  • Subscription rights (preemptive rights) go only to existing shareholders so they can keep their ownership percentage.
  • Subscription price is set below the market, so rights have intrinsic value and trade.
  • Life is short: typically 30–45 days.
  • Holder may exercise, sell, or let the right expire.
  • Exercise causes the company to issue new shares (dilutive). Holders who do not exercise are diluted.
  • Cum rights = stock still carries the right (before the ex-rights date). Ex-rights = right has been separated.
  • On a sale of a right, the broker acts as agent.

Exam traps

  • Rights are not offered to new investors.
  • Subscription price is below market. Warrants are priced above market at issuance.
  • Rights last days (30–45). Warrants last years.

Warrants

  • Long-term option to buy stock at a fixed exercise price, issued by the corporation, not the OCC.
  • Typical life 2–5 years; some are perpetual.
  • Attached to a bond or preferred as a sweetener, then often detached and traded alone.
  • Exercise price is set above the market at issuance. The warrant gains value if the stock later rises through that price.
  • Exercise issues new shares (dilutive). Listed options cleared by the OCC transfer existing shares.

  • Warrants are not OCC options.
  • Both rights and warrants dilute. Listed equity options do not create new shares.

Convertible Securities

  • Convertible preferred and convertible bonds exchange into a set number of common shares at the holder’s option. The issuer cannot force conversion.
  • They pay lower yields than non-convertible equivalents. The conversion privilege is the reason.
  • Conversion does not guarantee a profit. Compare the shares received with the convertible’s market value.

Conversion price Par ÷ conversion ratio
Conversion value Stock price × conversion ratio

Treasury Securities

  • Issued by the U.S. Treasury. Backed by full faith and credit. Treated as the risk-free benchmark (virtually zero credit risk).
  • Interest is taxed federally and exempt from state and local tax (the reverse of municipals).
  • Sold at auction. Competitive bidders name a yield and may be filled in part or shut out. Non-competitive bidders accept the auction yield and are guaranteed securities, up to $10 million per auction.
Type Maturity Coupon Issued at Feature
T-bills Up to 1 year: 4, 6, 8, 13, 17, 26, 52 weeks None Discount Return = discount earned
T-notes 2–10 years Semiannual Par Most actively traded
T-bonds 20–30 years Semiannual Par Highest rate risk among coupon Treasuries
TIPS 5, 10, or 30 years Semiannual, rate fixed Par Principal adjusts with CPI
STRIPS Varies, up to 30 years None Discount Stripped from notes, bonds, or TIPS
  • TIPS: coupon rate stays fixed. Principal moves with the CPI. Dollar interest = fixed rate × adjusted principal. At maturity the investor gets the greater of adjusted principal or original par (deflation floor).
  • TIPS example: 2% coupon, CPI +3%, principal $1,000 → $1,030. Annual interest 2% × $1,030 = $20.60 (was $20.00), paid semiannually.
  • STRIPS = Separate Trading of Registered Interest and Principal of Securities. Each coupon and the principal become a separate zero-coupon security.
  • A 10-year, $1,000, 4% note pays $20 every six months: 20 coupons + 1 principal = 21 STRIPS. The year-7 coupon strip pays only that $20.
  • STRIPS remove reinvestment risk (nothing to reinvest) and have very high interest-rate risk. Pension funds use them to match a known future liability.
  • Phantom income: taxable accounts accrue the discount each year on long-term STRIPS and on long-term zero-coupon corporates, with no cash until maturity.
  • T-bills are original-issue zeros of 1 year or less. Their discount is taxed as interest at maturity. They do not create phantom income. STRIPS are created from existing notes, bonds, or TIPS and can run to 30 years.

Exam traps

  • TIPS adjust principal, not the coupon rate.
  • T-bills and STRIPS are both zeros. Only long STRIPS (and long corporate zeros) create phantom income.
  • Non-competitive bidders are guaranteed fill. Competitive bidders can be shut out.
  • Treasury interest is state-tax-free and federally taxable.

Corporate Bonds

  • Issued by companies (debt, not stock). Semiannual coupon, par $1,000, principal at maturity.
  • Credit (default) risk. Interest is fully taxable at federal, state, and local levels.
  • Liquidation: secured → debentures → subordinated debentures → preferred → common.

  • Debenture means unsecured. Subordinated means lower priority and a higher yield.
  • Corporate interest is taxed at all three levels. Municipal interest generally is not taxed federally.
  • Zeros have the highest rate sensitivity at a given maturity, and they are taxed before any cash arrives.

Municipal Securities

  • Issued by states, cities, counties, school districts, and public authorities.
  • Interest is generally exempt from federal income tax. Capital gains are taxable.
  • Double-exempt (federal + state) if the buyer lives in the issuing state. Triple-exempt (federal + state + city) in some places, such as New York City.
  • Par is $1,000, same as corporates. Standard minimum denomination is $5,000 (five bonds).
  • A 5% tax-free yield is worth more to a high-bracket investor (source example: the 37% bracket) than the same taxable yield.
  • Private-activity interest can be pulled into the AMT. The taxpayer pays the higher of regular tax or AMT. AMT follows the use of proceeds, not the GO or revenue label, and it is a federal calculation.
  • A public project (a government-owned hospital) is not a private-activity bond. Proceeds that mainly benefit private users are.

Price and Interest Rate Relationship

  • Rates up, prices down. Rates down, prices up. This is arithmetic, not a tendency.
  • Existing coupons stay fixed. Price moves until the bond’s yield matches the new market rate.
  • Sensitivity is the holder’s problem (a sale before maturity). The issuer’s coupon does not reset.
Factor More price sensitivity Less price sensitivity
Maturity Longer Shorter
Coupon Lower Higher
  • A 30-year bond moves more than a 2-year note for the same rate change, if coupons match. If coupons differ, weigh both factors.
  • A 2% coupon moves more than an 8% coupon. Cash arriving sooner shortens effective duration.
  • At the same maturity, a zero has more rate risk than a coupon bond. The worst case is a long zero: a 20- or 30-year STRIP, which is more sensitive than a 30-year T-bond.
  • T-bills are zeros with very low rate risk because maturity is at most 1 year. A 26-week T-bill is far less sensitive than a 30-year STRIP or a 30-year T-bond.

Memory aid: See-saw: rates on one end, bond prices on the other.

Exam traps

  • Longer maturity and lower coupon mean more sensitivity.
  • Rank by maturity alone only when the coupons are the same.
  • The coupon rate does not change when market rates change.
  • Short zeros (T-bills) are not high-rate-risk bonds.

Calls and Puts

  • An option is a derivative: the right, not the duty, to buy or sell at a set price by a set time.
  • Buyer (holder) pays the premium and holds rights. Seller (writer) receives the premium and takes obligations.
  • Long = buyer. Short = writer. That label is not the same as shorting stock (borrow shares, sell, hope to repurchase lower).
  • Call = right to buy. Call buyer is bullish. Call writer must sell if assigned.
  • Put = right to sell. Put buyer is bearish. Put writer must buy if assigned.
  • A stock can fall to $0 and not below. That is why put max gain (buyer) and max loss (writer) equal strike − premium.

Exam traps

  • Long put and short call are both bearish. The put’s loss is the premium. The uncovered short call’s loss is unlimited.
  • Short-put max loss assumes the stock at $0, not a small decline.
  • “Short” an option means the writer. It does not mean the writer borrowed the option.

Option Contract Components

  • Strike (exercise) price is fixed for the life of the contract. Listed strikes are usually $1, $2.50, or $5 apart.
  • Premium = intrinsic value + time value. Each piece has a floor of $0.
  • Call intrinsic = market − strike, if positive; otherwise $0.
  • Put intrinsic = strike − market, if positive; otherwise $0.
  • Time value = premium − intrinsic value. More time left → more time value. Time decay reduces time value into expiration (volatility and dividends held constant on the exam). At expiration, time value is $0.
  • Sellers benefit from time decay. Buyers fight it.
  • Example: $50 call, stock $53 → intrinsic $3. If premium is $5, time value is $2.
  • An out-of-the-money option can still have a premium that is all time value.
  • Expiration is the last exercise date. After that the contract is worthless. Standard equity options expire on the third Friday of the expiration month (named like “January 50 call”).
  • Standard listed options are short-term, generally under a year. LEAPS (long-term equity anticipation securities) are listed options with 12 months or more until expiration. Same contract terms otherwise: 100 shares, premium quote × 100.
  • One equity contract = 100 shares. A $3 premium costs $300. Per-share profit or loss × 100 = contract dollars. Breakeven is a per-share stock price and is never multiplied by 100.

Exam traps

  • Premium is not the same number as intrinsic value.
  • Intrinsic value cannot be negative.
  • Do not multiply breakeven by 100. Do multiply the premium quote by 100 for the cash premium.

  • Put example, strike $50: stock $45 is ITM by $5; stock $50 is ATM; stock $55 is OTM by $5.

  • Check call vs. put before deciding which side of the strike is ITM.
  • ATM intrinsic value is $0.
  • Only ITM options are exercised at expiration.

  • Guarantor and central counterparty for listed options. Novation: buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer.

Exam traps

  • OCC assignment to firms is random, not “largest position first.”
  • The OCC guarantees contracts. It does not regulate them.

  • Covered call: if assigned, deliver shares already owned. It does not stop the stock from falling to $0; the premium only cushions that loss. It is an income strategy that gives up upside.
  • Naked call: if assigned, buy at the market and sell at the strike. Highest-risk options strategy. Firms may set margin above the regulatory minimum.

  • Covered put: short stock and short put. Assignment buys shares that close the short stock. The short-stock leg is why upside loss is unlimited.

  • A covered put is not a low-risk trade.
  • Covered calls may sit in a cash account. Naked calls and naked puts need margin.

  • Covered call caps the upside. Protective put caps the downside. Neither removes all risk.

Introduction

Open-End Funds (Mutual Funds)

  • Continuously create shares on purchase and retire them on redemption. No fixed share count.
  • Buy from and sell back to the fund, not on an exchange.
  • Cannot be bought on margin or sold short.
  • No market, limit, or stop orders. Only a purchase or redemption request.

NAV (once daily, after the close):

NAV = (total assets − total liabilities) / shares outstanding

Worked: $98 million net assets / 5 million shares = $19.60.

Forward pricing. The NAV is not known when the order is entered. There is no way to lock a price.

Order time Price
Before 4:00 PM ET That day’s closing NAV
After 4:00 PM ET Next business day’s closing NAV

POP = NAV + sales charge. No-load: POP = NAV.

  • Bought at POP. Redeemed at NAV.
  • Worked: NAV $20.00 + sales charge $1.05 = POP $21.05. Pay $21.05 to buy; receive $20.00 to redeem.
  • Sales-charge percent is of POP, not of NAV: (POP − NAV) / POP.
  • The load comes out of the check. $10,000 at a 5% front-end load → $500 charge, $9,500 invested. It is not added on top of $10,000.

Redemption: the fund must pay within 7 calendar days of a proper request (not business days).

Closed-End Funds

  • Fixed shares through an IPO, then trade on NYSE or Nasdaq all day.
  • The fund does not redeem. Investors sell to other investors.
  • “Closed-end” means the share count is fixed. It does not mean the fund is closed to new buyers. After the IPO you buy on the exchange.
  • They are investment companies, not operating-company stocks.
  • Exchange-traded shares: margin and short sales allowed. Market, limit, and stop orders allowed.
  • Leverage: open-end funds can borrow from banks within limits. Closed-end funds have broader options, including preferred shares (and debt), still subject to limits. “Open-end funds cannot use any leverage” is too broad.

Unit Investment Trusts

  • Fixed portfolio assembled at creation (typically bonds, sometimes stocks). Buy-and-hold.
  • No investment adviser, no board, no management fee. A trustee runs it.
  • Charges a creation/sales charge and a trustee fee instead.
  • Stated termination date (bond UITs wind down as holdings mature or are sold). Not perpetual.
  • Issues redeemable units of beneficial interest. Income passes through.
  • Proceeds of a sale or maturity are distributed, never reinvested (opposite of a typical mutual fund).
  • Separate ICA category, not a management company.

  • “Held to maturity” is each bond, not one shared end date. A bond UIT can hold different maturities. Principal comes back as each bond matures, not all at once.
  • Many sponsors voluntarily make a secondary market (buy units back and resell near NAV). That is not exchange trading: no negotiated price, no premium or discount, and the sponsor can stop at any time.
  • The trust must still redeem units at NAV, so there is always an exit.

Breakpoints

Volume discounts on the front-end percentage for Class A only. Never B or C. Each fund sets its own schedule in the prospectus. There is no universal schedule. They cut the percent, not a flat dollar amount.

Illustrative schedule (a fund’s own table may differ):

Amount Sales charge

$25,000–$49,999 5.00%
$50,000–$99,999 4.50%

Breakpoint sale: the representative sells just under a breakpoint and does not disclose the discount. Violation by the representative, even with no intent. The investor is not the violator.

Worked: customer wants $49,500 and the next breakpoint is $50,000. The rep must say that $500 more gets the lower charge. Failing to say so is a breakpoint sale.

Rights of Accumulation

Count existing holdings at current NAV (not original cost) plus the new purchase toward a Class A breakpoint.

  • Same fund family (fund complex): funds with the same sponsor / same adviser. Vanguard funds combine with each other. Fidelity funds combine with each other.
  • Not the same family: Vanguard 500 and Fidelity 500, even with nearly identical holdings.
  • Not the same family: a Fidelity fund and an iShares ETF merely held in a Fidelity brokerage account. Same broker ≠ same family.
  • Accounts that usually combine: individual, joint, custodial, and typically immediate family.
  • The reduced charge applies to the new purchase only. Prior loads are not refunded.
  • One purchase today, no future schedule → ROA, not an LOI.
  • Because the family shares a sponsor, investors can also exchange inside the family at NAV with no new sales charge, and reinvest distributions across the family.

Worked: $40,000 already in the fund + $15,000 new = $55,000, which clears the $50,000 breakpoint. The lower charge hits the new $15,000 only.

ROA value moves with the market: invested $50,000, now worth $35,000 → credit is $35,000. Grew to $60,000 → credit is $60,000.

Variable Contracts

A variable annuity is both an insurance product and a security.

  • Regulated by the SEC and by state insurance regulators.
  • Seller needs a securities license (Series 6 or Series 7) and a state insurance license.
  • Sold by prospectus.
  • Money goes into separate-account sub-accounts that work like mutual funds.
  • Returns are not guaranteed.
  • Fixed annuities are not securities. Only variable annuities are.
  • No contribution limits (unlike an IRA or 401(k)).

Accumulation

  • Lump sum or periodic payments. Growth is tax-deferred.
  • Withdrawals before annuitization are LIFO: earnings come out first and are ordinary income. Basis is reached only after all earnings are out. Do not use FIFO.
  • Worked: $50,000 invested, contract now $70,000 ($20,000 earnings). A $20,000 withdrawal is 100% earnings, fully taxable.
  • Before age 59½: 10% IRS penalty on the taxable (earnings) portion, plus ordinary income tax on that same portion. Same age rule as IRAs.

Annuitization

  • Stream of payments, taxed as ordinary income, not capital gains, even if sub-accounts hold stocks.
  • Generally irrevocable once annuitized.
Payout What the beneficiary gets

Surrender charges: CDSC-like fees in the surrender period, typically declining over 5–10 years. Same idea as a Class B CDSC, different product. They can still apply on a 1035 exchange.

Other features

  • Death benefit in accumulation: beneficiary gets at least the greater of amount invested or current account value.
  • 1035 exchange: tax-free exchange of annuity for annuity, or life insurance for an annuity. Surrender charges on the old contract may still apply.

  • That review is within 7 business days of a complete application. (Mutual-fund redemption is 7 calendar days. Do not swap them.)
  • Exchanging one VA for another inside 36 months gets extra FINRA scrutiny because of surrender charges on the old contract.

  • ICA types are three. Face-amount certificates still count. UITs are not management companies.
  • “Diversified” is the 75% / 5% / 10% test, not “owns many stocks.”
  • Buy at POP, redeem at NAV. Forward pricing: you do not know the price when you order. Cutoff is 4:00 PM ET.
  • Redemption deadline is 7 calendar days. VA principal review is 7 business days.
  • Only closed-end funds trade at a premium or discount, and most trade at a discount. Open-end can still borrow from banks. “Closed-end” ≠ closed to buyers.
  • Mutual funds: no margin, no short sales. Closed-end funds and ETPs: both allowed.
  • UIT: trustee, no management fee, proceeds distributed not reinvested, sponsor secondary market is not an exchange, redemption at NAV still required.
  • Balanced = stocks and bonds. Growth and income = still all equity.
  • Only Class A gets breakpoints, LOI, and ROA. 8.5% is of POP. Ceilings drop to 8.0% / 7.75% / 7.25% if a required feature is missing. Dividend reinvestment is not a condition of the 8.5% max.
  • No-load can still have a 12b-1 up to 0.25%. Total 12b-1 cap is 1.00% = 0.75% + 0.25%. Loads are not in the expense ratio.
  • Breakpoint sale is the rep’s violation, even without intent. Example: $49,500 vs $50,000.
  • ROA uses current NAV, same fund family, new purchases only, no refund of old loads. Same brokerage account does not create a fund family. Today’s purchase = ROA. A pledge over time = LOI (13 months, backdate 90 days, escrow, not binding).
  • Same-family fund exchange can be free of a new load and still be taxable. A 1035 exchange is the tax-free insurance swap.
  • Variable annuities: ordinary income, LIFO (earnings first), 10% before 59½ on the taxable part only. Fixed annuities are not securities. 1035 does not erase surrender charges. Extra scrutiny inside 36 months.
  • Prospectus before or at sale. SAI only if asked.

Municipal Fund Securities

Introduction

State and local programs for education, disability expenses, and government cash. Tested points: product type, tax treatment, who controls the account, and who regulates the sale.

Municipal Fund Securities Overview

  • Shares or units in a pooled program issued by a state, locality, or agency. Not municipal bonds. Not debt.
  • A municipal bond is a loan to a government. A municipal fund security is a government-run pool.
  • Not registered investment companies under the 1940 Act.
  • Exempt from SEC registration (government-securities exemption). The exemption is on the product. Dealers who sell them still follow MSRB rules.
  • The MSRB defines the category and writes the sales rules. It regulates dealers, not issuers.
  • Enforcement: FINRA, the SEC, and federal bank regulators (for bank dealers).
  • Dealers must be MSRB-registered to sell them. Rules apply to adviser-sold plans. Direct-sold plans (bought from the state) have no dealer, so MSRB rules do not apply.

If the question asks who regulates these products, the answer is the MSRB.

529 Plans

Qualified tuition programs under the Internal Revenue Code, set up by a state or state agency. Every state and D.C. offers at least one. Two types.

Savings plans (the common one)

  • Mutual-fund-like portfolios (structurally similar to variable-annuity sub-accounts).
  • Value fluctuates. Returns are not guaranteed.
  • Usable at any eligible school nationwide, not just in-state.
  • Often age-based portfolios that get more conservative by themselves. The owner does not have to rebalance. Static portfolios and single funds may also be offered.

Qualified expenses:

  • Tuition and fees.
  • Room and board if the student is at least half-time.
  • Books, supplies, equipment, including computers.
  • K–12 qualified expenses up to $20,000 per year. No annual cap on qualified higher-education expenses.
  • Student-loan repayment up to $10,000 lifetime per borrower (not per year).

Prepaid tuition plans

  • Buy future tuition credits or units, often at in-state public schools. The contract is built to address tuition inflation. Not a market portfolio, so there is no selected-portfolio market return.
  • State backing is not universal. Read the plan. Some are state-backed; some are not.
  • FDIC and SIPC do not guarantee the tuition benefit.
  • Usually tuition and mandatory fees only. Most do not cover room, board, or books.
  • Full value is generally at participating schools. If the student goes elsewhere, the plan can still be applied to tuition, but the dollar amount follows the plan’s formula.
  • Fewer states offer them; many are closed to new enrollees. No investment choices.

Gifts and superfunding

  • A contribution is a completed gift.
  • Annual gift-tax exclusion: $19,000 per beneficiary (2026). Inside the exclusion, no gift-tax return.
  • Superfunding: contribute up to 5× the annual exclusion and elect to spread it over 5 years.
  • 2026: up to $95,000 per beneficiary, or $190,000 for a married couple.
  • File IRS Form 709 in the first year.
  • That election uses up the annual exclusion for that beneficiary for all 5 years. Other gifts to the same person in those years are taxable gifts and use the lifetime exemption. Extra gifts are still allowed; they are just not sheltered by the annual exclusion.
  • If the donor dies during the 5 years, the portion assigned to years after death is pulled back into the estate.

Owner vs. Beneficiary

Owner (often a parent or grandparent; can be anyone):

  • Controls investments and withdrawals. Retains ownership at all times, including after the beneficiary turns 18 or 21.
  • Can change the beneficiary to a family member with no tax hit.
  • Can take the money back, but earnings are then ordinary income plus the 10% penalty.
  • A change to a non-family member is treated as a non-qualified distribution (income tax + 10% on earnings).

Family members who can be the new beneficiary: siblings (including step and half), parents and step-parents, grandparents and other ancestors, children, step-children, and other descendants, nieces and nephews, aunts and uncles, first cousins, in-laws (son-, daughter-, parent-, sibling-in-law), and the spouse of any of those.

Beneficiary: the student. No control. Does not own the assets. Several 529 accounts may name the same beneficiary.

ABLE Accounts

Achieving a Better Life Experience accounts. ABLE Act of 2014. Tax-advantaged savings that supplement SSI and Medicaid; they do not replace those benefits. A municipal fund security.

SSI vs Medicaid while the person is alive

  • SSI is suspended, not terminated, when the ABLE balance exceeds $100,000 (a balance of $100,001 or more). Benefits resume automatically when the balance falls back under $100,000. “Terminated” would mean a new application. That is not the rule.
  • Medicaid is not affected by the balance during life.

At death

  • Only Medicaid benefits paid after the account was opened. Not benefits since birth. Not SSI, SNAP, or other programs.

Exam traps

  • Municipal fund security = a pool, not a bond. Regulator of the dealers is the MSRB. Issuers are outside MSRB rules. Product is SEC-registration exempt; the seller is not exempt from MSRB rules. Not a 1940 Act company.
  • MSRB covers adviser-sold plans only. A dealer who recommends one must say a cheaper direct-sold plan may exist.
  • Underwriter filings: most data semi-annual; performance annual. Public site is EMMA.
  • Savings plans are not guaranteed and can be used at any eligible school. Prepaid guarantees vary by plan. Neither FDIC nor SIPC guarantees prepaid tuition. Rapid-fire shorthand “prepaid = state-guaranteed” overstates the lesson: backing is plan-specific.
  • K–12 cap is $20,000 per year. Student loans are $10,000 lifetime per borrower. Higher-education qualified costs have no annual cap. Room and board needs half-time enrollment.
  • No federal deduction on contributions. No income limit and no age limit (that pair distinguishes a 529 from a Coverdell: Coverdell is $2,000/year, income phase-out, beneficiary under 18).
  • 10% on earnings only. Scholarship, academy, death, and disability waive the penalty, not the income tax. Out-of-state school is not a penalty exception for a savings plan.
  • Superfunding ($95,000 / $190,000, Form 709 in year 1) consumes that beneficiary’s annual exclusion for 5 years. Death during the window pulls unused years into the estate.
  • Owner controls a 529 even after 18 or 21. UGMA/UTMA gifts are irrevocable and become the minor’s at majority. Non-family beneficiary change is a non-qualified distribution.
  • Roth rollover needs all of: 15 years open, 5-year seasoning, annual Roth limit, $35,000 lifetime, beneficiary is the Roth owner, trustee-to-trustee. Roth income limits do not apply.
  • ABLE: onset before 46 (was 26), 12 months, one account, beneficiary owns it, $20,000 (2026) and that figure is no longer the gift exclusion. SSI suspended over $100,000. Medicaid untouched during life. Payback at death is Medicaid only, and only post-opening benefits.
  • LGIP: governments only, stable $1.00 is not guaranteed and not FDIC.

Exchange-Traded Funds

  • Most ETFs are registered investment companies (open-end funds or UITs) under the 1940 Act.
  • Commodity and currency ETFs (gold, oil) are often grantor trusts or commodity pools. Those are not registered investment companies. If the question is “1940 Act registration,” the usual answer is a securities ETF, not an ETN, and not automatically every commodity ETF.
  • Shares = ownership of a portfolio (stocks, bonds, commodities, and so on).
  • Most are passive index trackers. Some are active.

Creation / redemption (what keeps price near NAV):

  • Authorized participants (large institutions or broker-dealers) deal with the issuer.
  • They create and redeem creation units, typically 10,000–100,000 shares.
  • Creation: AP delivers the basket, receives ETF shares.
  • Redemption: AP delivers ETF shares, receives the basket.
  • Premium (price > NAV): APs buy the cheaper basket, create shares, sell the expensive ETF.
  • Discount (price < NAV): APs buy the cheap ETF, redeem for the richer basket.
  • Individuals do not create or redeem. They buy and sell on the secondary market like a stock.

Tax: creation and redemption are usually in-kind. In-kind transfer is not a taxable event at the fund. ETFs therefore distribute far fewer capital gains than mutual funds. Most mutual funds pay an annual capital-gains distribution; only a small share of ETFs do. The efficiency comes from in-kind exchange, not from low turnover alone. The investor’s own tax is deferred until that investor sells.

Advantages vs mutual funds: intraday trading; low expense ratios (passive often 0.03%–0.20%); tax efficiency; most ETFs publish holdings daily; no minimum (as little as 1 share, or a fraction); no sales loads (a brokerage commission may still apply).

Disadvantages: commissions (many brokers now $0); bid-ask spread on every trade; possible premium or discount to NAV; no breakpoints, no LOI, no ROA (those are Class A mutual-fund features).

Exchange-Traded Notes

  • Unsecured senior debt of a bank. Not an investment company. Not the 1940 Act.
  • Registered under the Securities Act of 1933.
  • Promise a return linked to an index. Hold no securities.
  • The return is a contractual promise, so no tracking error.
  • Trade on exchanges. Typical maturity 10–30 years. ETFs do not mature.
  • Often no dividends or interest; the return shows up at sale or maturity. That can make some strategies more tax-efficient than an ETF.
  • Issuer credit risk is the tested risk. If the bank defaults, the investor can lose the entire investment even if the index is fine.
  • September 2008: Lehman Brothers ETN holders lost their investment when the bank failed. The index did not have to fail with it.

“No tracking error” is paid for with credit risk. It is not a free feature.

Exam traps

  • Credit risk, default, or issuer bankruptcy → ETN. Tracking error → ETF. Do not swap them.
  • 1940 Act = most ETFs. 1933 Act = ETNs. Commodity/currency ETFs may be grantor trusts or commodity pools and not 1940 Act funds.
  • ETNs hold nothing, mature in 10–30 years, and have no tracking error only because the bank promised the return. Lehman (September 2008) is the credit-risk example.
  • Individuals do not use creation units (10,000–100,000 shares). APs do. In-kind is why ETFs distribute fewer capital gains.
  • ETFs have no breakpoints, LOI, ROA, or (for exam purposes) 12b-1 fees. Both ETFs and ETNs can be margined and sold short; mutual funds cannot.
  • Unspecified “ETF” = passive. Passive aims to match, and a small tracking error is normal. Active aims to beat, costs more (0.20%–0.75% vs 0.03%–0.20%), and still usually costs less than an active mutual fund.
  • $0 commission does not remove the spread. Dollar-cost averaging can make a no-load mutual fund cheaper than an ETF. Breakpoints exist only on Class A mutual funds.

Alternative Investments

Introduction

  • Products: Direct Participation Programs (DPPs), Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs), hedge funds.
  • Shared trade-off: tax or return features come with weak liquidity and tighter investor eligibility.
  • Exam habit: match structure, tax form, liquidity, and who can buy.

DPP Overview

  • A Direct Participation Program (DPP) is a business venture that passes income, gains, losses, deductions, and credits straight to investors.
  • Defining feature: no corporate-level tax. Investors feel the cash flow and the tax result directly.
  • FINRA suitability and compensation rules govern DPPs.
  • Annual tax form: K-1. C-corporation shareholders get a 1099-DIV.
Feature C corporation DPP (pass-through)
Entity-level tax Yes, at the corporate rate No
Investor-level tax Dividends taxed again Income hits the personal return
Double taxation Yes No
Tax form 1099-DIV K-1
  • C-corp profits are taxed twice: once at the company, again when paid out as dividends. A DPP is taxed once, on the investor’s return.
  • DPPs pass losses through as well as income. Passive-activity rules still apply (see DPP Key Characteristics).
  • Common purposes: real estate, oil and gas, equipment leasing, agriculture.

Limited Partnerships

  • The limited partnership is the most common DPP structure. Two partner types.
Feature General partner (GP) Limited partner (LP)
Liability Unlimited personal liability Limited to amount invested plus recourse debt
Management All business decisions No active management
Day-to-day Runs the business Passive investor
Fiduciary duty Owes a duty to the LPs None
Tax form K-1 K-1
Exit / removal Removed only with majority LP consent Withdraws under the agreement
  • GP: day-to-day control, all investment decisions, fiduciary duty, unlimited liability for partnership debts. Removal takes a majority vote of LPs. A GP cannot be removed by one LP acting alone.
  • LP: supplies capital, stays passive, liability capped at capital invested plus recourse debt, annual K-1 (share of income, losses, deductions).
  • LP losses are passive. They offset other passive income. They do not offset wages, salaries, or portfolio income.
  • Management boundary: an LP who participates in management can be reclassified as a GP and pick up unlimited personal liability.
  • Memory aid: liability tracks control. Limited liability travels with limited management rights. Unlimited liability travels with unlimited authority. LPs invest money. GPs make decisions.

Tenants in Common (TIC)

  • TIC is another DPP structure: co-ownership of real estate, not a partnership and not a separate legal entity.
  • Each investor holds an undivided fractional interest and a separate deed.
  • Ownership shares do not have to be equal. A 99% / 1% split is still a valid TIC.
  • Each owner reports that owner’s income and expenses on the owner’s own tax return. TIC does not file a partnership return.
  • Each owner may sell, transfer, or bequeath that owner’s interest with no consent from the other co-owners.
  • No right of survivorship. At death the interest passes through the estate (probate).
  • Joint tenancy with right of survivorship (JTWROS) is the other pattern: the survivor takes the interest automatically. TIC does not work that way.

DPP Key Characteristics

The exam uses 3 traits to identify a DPP:

  • Pass-through tax (no double tax; K-1).
  • Illiquid (no active secondary market; unlisted).
  • Suitability rules.

Tax limits on losses

  • Passive-activity rules: DPP losses are passive. They generally offset passive income only. Wages, salaries, and portfolio (investment) income stay off-limits. Losses are not an unlimited tax shelter.
  • At-risk rules: deductible losses stop at the amount the investor has at risk — the investment plus recourse debt.

Illiquidity

  • No active secondary market. Interests are hard to sell or transfer.
  • Plan to hold for the life of the program, often 7 to 12+ years.
  • Unlisted. DPP interests do not trade on an exchange.

FINRA suitability

  • Suitable only for investors who can bear the economic risk.
  • Investors must meet net worth and income requirements (this lesson states no dollar floor).
  • The rep needs reasonable grounds that the specific program fits the specific customer.
  • The rep must do due diligence on the program before recommending it.
  • Compensation to underwriters and affiliates must be fair and reasonable.

REIT Overview

  • A Real Estate Investment Trust (REIT) owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate.
  • Legal form: trust or corporation. A typical DPP is a partnership. A REIT is not.
  • Congress created REITs in 1960 so individuals could own a slice of large real-estate portfolios without buying, managing, or financing property themselves.
  • 90% distribution rule: distribute at least 90% of taxable income as dividends. Meeting the rule avoids corporate-level tax on the distributed income (no double tax on that slice).
  • REIT dividends are taxed as ordinary income. Regular stock dividends can be qualified and taxed at the lower capital-gains rate.
  • Why the rates differ: a qualified dividend requires the paying corporation to have already paid corporate tax. A REIT that uses the 90% rule has not paid that corporate tax on distributed income, so the shareholder pays ordinary rates.
  • Part of a distribution may be return of capital: tax-deferred, and it reduces cost basis.
Feature REIT dividends Regular stock dividends
Shareholder rate Ordinary income Qualified dividend rate (lower)
Why No entity tax on distributed income (dividends-paid deduction) Earnings already taxed at the corporation
Entity-level tax None on income that is distributed, if the 90% test is met Corporate tax paid first

Three types by what they invest in. Match income source to type.

Type What they do Income source Risk Inflation
Equity REIT Own and operate property (landlord) Rent + property appreciation Moderate Yes — rents and values tend to rise with inflation
Mortgage REIT (mREIT) Lend to property owners or hold mortgage-backed securities (MBS). They do not own the property Interest spread (borrow cost vs. lend rate) Higher No — fixed-rate assets
Hybrid REIT Own some property and hold mortgages Rent and interest Moderate to high Mixed; only the equity slice behaves like an equity REIT

  • mREITs are highly rate-sensitive. Rates up → borrowing costs up and existing mortgage holdings fall in value. They tend to do best in stable or falling rate environments.

  • Hybrid risk sits between equity and mortgage and diversifies the income sources.

Listed

  • Bought and sold daily like stock.
  • Price is supply and demand. It is not mutual-fund NAV.
  • Reporting includes the annual 10-K and quarterly 10-Q.
  • Most liquid REIT.

Public non-traded (the category the exam presses)

  • SEC-registered and still highly illiquid. “Public” is not “liquid.”
  • Upfront commissions and offering costs can exceed 10–15%.
  • Worked fee: 15% of $10,000 = $1,500 in front-end cost, so $8,500 is the capital that goes to work.
  • NAV is not updated daily. The stated value can be stale.
  • Redemption programs are limited and the REIT can suspend them.
  • Expect to hold 8 years or more before a liquidity event (listing, sale, or wind-down). FINRA has issued an investor alert on this illiquidity.
  • Distributions may be funded by borrowed money or return of principal, not only operating income. A steady yield can mislead.

Private

  • Sold under Regulation D. Not SEC-registered.
  • Least disclosure, least liquidity, least transparency.
  • Buyers must meet private-placement eligibility.

Liquidity rule: listing status sets liquidity. REIT type does not. A non-traded equity REIT is illiquid. A listed mortgage REIT is liquid.

REIT Tax Treatment

  • Distribute 90% or more of taxable income → the REIT is not taxed on the income it distributes.
  • Mechanism: the dividends-paid deduction. This is a different path from DPP pass-through. A REIT does not issue a K-1 and does not pass losses through to investors.
  • Income the REIT retains is still taxed at the entity level.
  • Trade-off: shareholders pay higher (ordinary) rates on most distributions.
Distribution Tax when received Cost basis
Ordinary dividends Ordinary income Unchanged
Capital-gains distributions Capital-gains rates Unchanged
Return of capital Tax-deferred (not taxed on receipt) Reduced
  • Most of the cash is ordinary income, because that income was never taxed at the REIT.
  • Return of capital happens when cash distributed exceeds taxable income. Depreciation is a non-cash expense: it cuts taxable income and does not cut cash. The excess cash is treated as handing the investor back part of the original investment.
  • Lower basis → a larger taxable gain when the shares are sold. The tax is deferred, not erased.
  • Qualified-dividend test: the payer must already have paid corporate tax. REITs using the 90% rule fail that test, so the preferential rate does not apply.

REIT Investment Characteristics

Benefits

  • Real-estate diversification without owning, managing, or financing the property.
  • Income from the mandatory 90% payout. A REIT that keeps earnings like a normal corporation loses the dividends-paid deduction and entity-level shelter on that income.
  • Professional management.
  • Listed REITs: buy in for the price of one share. No large down payment.

Risks

  • Interest-rate risk: every REIT feels rates. Mortgage REITs feel them most (funding cost up; mortgage portfolio value down).
  • Market risk: listed REITs trade like stocks and can be volatile.
  • Liquidity risk: non-traded and private REITs are hard to sell.
  • Fee risk: non-traded upfront costs can consume returns (see the 10–15% band).
  • Distribution risk: some non-traded payouts are borrowed funds or return of capital, not operating income.

Fit

  • Equity REIT: partial inflation hedge.
  • Mortgage REIT: better when rates are stable or falling.
  • Listed: real estate plus stock-market liquidity.
  • Non-traded: long horizon and tolerance for illiquidity.

Hedge Fund Overview

  • A private investment pool, sold under a private-offering exemption.
  • Form: limited partnership or LLC. Manager is the GP. Investors are LPs.
  • Not registered with the SEC as an investment company. Exempt from the Investment Company Act of 1940 under a private-fund exemption.
  • The fund’s exemption does not cover the manager. The adviser may still have to register under the Investment Advisers Act.
  • Generally limited to accredited or qualified investors. This lesson gives dollar tests only for accredited investors:
  • Net worth exceeding $1 million, excluding the primary residence, or
  • Income exceeding $200,000 ($300,000 with a spouse) in each of the past 2 years.

Hedge Fund Characteristics

  • Typical minimum: $250,000 to $1 million or more. Combined with accredited/qualified status, that screens out most retail investors.
  • Same partnership tax shape as a DPP: GP decides, LPs supply capital and share profits and losses, pass-through, K-1 (the fund itself pays no entity-level tax). GP owes a fiduciary duty.

“2 and 20”

Fee Typical rate Base When
Management fee 2% per year All assets under management Charged whether the fund makes money or not
Performance fee (carried interest / incentive fee) 20% of profits Net gains above a benchmark or hurdle Only when the fund is profitable
  • Worked example on $1,000,000: management fee = $1,000,000 × 2% = $20,000. If the fund gains $100,000, performance fee = $100,000 × 20% = $20,000. Total fees = $40,000.
  • High-water mark: the performance fee applies only to gains above the prior peak NAV. The manager is not paid a second performance fee for climbing back through old losses.

Private equity inside the fund

  • Stakes in companies that are not publicly traded: venture capital, leveraged buyouts (LBOs), distressed debt.
  • Very long holding periods, highly illiquid, no active market. Skill of the manager drives the result.

Lock-ups and liquidity

  • Lock-up: no right to redeem, often 1 to 2 years (sometimes longer).
  • After the lock-up, redemptions only in windows, often quarterly or annually.
  • Advance notice of 30 to 90 days.
  • No active secondary market.
  • Gates: a cap on how much of the fund can be redeemed in a period, often used in a stressed market. Investors can be stuck in a downturn. Lock-ups also block panic selling.

Hedge Fund Risks

  • Limited transparency: far less disclosure than a mutual fund. No daily NAV. No standardized performance reporting. Holdings and real-time results can be opaque.
  • Key-person risk (older name: key-man risk): results depend on the manager’s judgment. Departure or a bad decision can hit returns hard. This is active management, not an index.
  • Leverage risk: amplifies gains and losses; can wipe out more than invested capital; margin calls; can create systemic risk.
  • Liquidity risk: lock-ups (typically 1–2 years), limited windows, notice, gates, no secondary market. Capital can be trapped in a crisis.
  • Lack of regulation: fewer investor protections. The fund is not registered as an investment company. No limits on leverage, concentration, or strategy. Limited recourse.
  • Valuation risk: private companies, distressed debt, and real estate are hard to price. The manager’s discretion in marking illiquid holdings is a conflict. Stale marks can misstate performance.

Exam traps

  • See pass-through tax, no secondary market, and a suitability screen together → it is a DPP.
  • Both GP and LP get a K-1. The 1099-DIV distractor belongs to C-corp dividends.
  • Passive DPP losses offset passive income only. The distractor lets them offset wages or portfolio income.
  • At-risk cap = amount actually at risk (investment plus recourse debt), not an unlimited shelter.
  • An LP who starts managing the business can be treated as a GP and take unlimited liability. Limited liability and limited control stay paired.
  • GP removal needs a majority of LPs, not a unilateral firing.
  • TIC shares may be unequal, and a co-owner sells without consent. At death the interest goes through probate. JTWROS is the survivorship form.
  • TIC owners report on their own returns. There is no partnership return.
  • REIT legal form is trust or corporation (1960). It is not a DPP partnership, it does not issue a K-1, and it does not pass losses through.
  • The REIT tax test in this lesson is 90% of taxable income. Retained income is still entity-taxed. The tool is the dividends-paid deduction.
  • REIT dividends = ordinary income. Qualified rates require corporate tax already paid by the issuer.
  • Return of capital is tax-deferred and cuts basis, so the gain at sale is larger. It is not free money. Depreciation (non-cash) is why cash can exceed taxable income.
  • Three investment types, not two. Rent → equity. Interest spread / MBS → mortgage. Both → hybrid.
  • Inflation protection is an equity REIT point. Mortgage REITs lose value when rates rise.
  • “SEC-registered” is not “liquid.” Public non-traded REITs are registered, illiquid, and often held 8+ years. Listed prices come from supply and demand, not daily NAV.
  • Non-traded upfront fees can exceed 10–15% ($10,000 × 15% = $1,500 left out of the portfolio; $8,500 works). Payouts may be debt or return of capital.
  • Private REITs use Regulation D and are unregistered, with minimal disclosure.
  • Listing status sets liquidity. Strategy type sets income and inflation behavior.
  • Hedge funds are exempt from 1940 Act registration as an investment company. The manager may still register as an adviser.
  • No daily NAV, no on-demand redemption, no leverage cap, unrestricted short selling. Short-sale losses are theoretically unlimited.
  • Accredited tests: net worth over $1 million excluding the home, or income over $200,000 / $300,000 joint in each of the last 2 years. “Any investor” is the distractor.
  • 2% of all assets every year, plus 20% of profits. High-water mark blocks a second performance fee on recovered losses.
  • During the lock-up there is no redemption right. Gates can tighten withdrawals after that. Private-equity sleeves inside the fund are not quickly sellable.
  • “Hedge” names the old long/short idea. It does not mean conservative.

Investment Risks

Why this order. Market-wide risks, then risks that hit one issuer or one holding, then the three tools.

Introduction

  • The exam asks which risk attaches to which security, how risks relate, and which tool manages which risk.

Risk Overview

  • Risk = the chance the actual return differs from the expected return.
  • Risk and return move together. Higher expected return means accepting higher risk. There is no high-return, low-risk shortcut.
  • Nothing is truly risk-free. Treasuries are the closest benchmark and are free of credit (default) risk. They still carry inflation risk and interest-rate risk. They are free of capital risk only if held to maturity.

Market Risk (Systematic Risk)

  • Market risk = systematic risk = non-diversifiable risk. The whole market declines and pulls most securities with it, regardless of quality.
  • Drivers: broad economic, political, or social forces. A portfolio of 1,000 stocks still has it.
  • Events: recessions, wars, geopolitical conflict, pandemics, Federal Reserve rate changes, inflation spikes, major tax or trade policy.
  • A Fed rate move is systematic. It hits bond prices across issuers, not one company.
  • In a deep downturn, stocks, real estate, and corporate bonds can fall together.
  • Tools that can reduce it: hedging with index puts, and asset allocation into other asset classes. Adding more stocks addresses company risk instead.

Beta measures sensitivity to the market. The benchmark (S&P 500) beta is 1.0, not 0.

Beta Meaning Example in the lesson
1.0 Moves with the market S&P 500 index fund
> 1.0 More volatile Tech. Beta 1.3 ≈ 30% more volatile
< 1.0 Less volatile Utilities. Beta 0.6 ≈ 40% less volatile
0 No correlation with the market Some alternatives
Negative Tends to move opposite the chosen benchmark Rises when the benchmark falls
  • Beta 1.5: market −10% → stock tends to −15%. Beta 0.5: market −10% → stock tends to −5%. Higher beta amplifies both directions.
  • Risk that remains after full diversification: systematic / market risk.

Interest Rate Risk

  • Bond prices and interest rates move inversely. Rates up → prices down. Rates down → prices up.
  • Reason: a 3% bond is worth less at par once new bonds pay 5%. The holder sells at a discount.
  • Two factors make it worse: longer maturity and lower coupon.
  • A 30-year bond loses more value on a 1% rate rise than a 5-year bond.
  • A 2% coupon loses more than a 6% coupon of the same maturity.
  • Zero-coupon bonds have the most interest-rate risk. No coupon cash flow. All of the return is the discount to par. Duration equals maturity (the longest duration a bond of that maturity can have). A small rate change moves the price a lot.
  • Duration = price sensitivity to rate changes. Higher duration = more volatility. A duration of 7 falls about 7% in price if rates rise 1%.
  • High-coupon bonds have shorter duration because large coupons pull cash forward.
  • Longest-sensitivity case in the lesson: a 30-year zero.

Inflation Risk (Purchasing Power Risk)

  • Inflation risk (purchasing power risk): rising prices cut the real return.
  • Real return = nominal return − inflation.
  • A bond yielding 3% with inflation at 4% loses purchasing power. Payments still arrive; each dollar buys less.
  • A bond paying $50 a year buys fewer goods if milk goes from $4 to $6. At 10% inflation that $50 is worth much less in real terms than at 1% inflation. The nominal coupon does not change.
Security Inflation risk Why
Cash / savings Highest Yields rarely keep up
Long-term fixed-rate bonds High Payments locked for decades
Short-term bonds / T-bills Moderate Reprice sooner
TIPS Very low Principal adjusts semiannually with the CPI
Equities Lower Earnings can rise with prices
Real estate / REITs Lower Values and rents tend to rise with inflation
Commodities and other real assets Lower Can act as an inflation hedge
  • Fixed income is the vulnerable side, especially long bonds. Equities and real assets are the protected side. TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) adjust principal to the CPI.

Reinvestment Risk

  • Cash flows (coupons, returned principal) must be reinvested at lower rates. It bites when rates are falling. The scheduled payment still arrives; the new rate does not match the old one.
  • High reinvestment risk: callable bonds (highest — the issuer calls when rates drop and hands principal back), MBS (homeowners refinance), high-coupon bonds (more cash to reinvest).
  • Zero-coupon bonds have zero reinvestment risk. Nothing is paid until maturity, so the purchase discount locks the return. They still have maximum interest-rate risk. Any coupon bond has some reinvestment risk.
Interest-rate risk Reinvestment risk
Rates rise Prices fall (bad for the holder who sells) New cash is invested at higher rates (good)
Rates fall Prices rise (good) New cash is invested at lower rates (bad)
Worst instruments Long maturity, low coupon, zeros High coupon, callable bonds, MBS
Trigger Rates rise Rates fall
  • The two risks move in opposite directions.

Prepayment Risk

  • Borrowers repay principal earlier than scheduled. Main products: MBS and callable bonds.
  • Rates fall → homeowners refinance → mortgages pay off early → MBS investors get principal back and reinvest at the new lower rate. The higher coupons end sooner. Faster prepayments shorten the MBS’s life.
  • Extension risk is the other side. Rates rise → refinancing stops → prepayments slow → the MBS lives longer than expected → the investor holds a below-market yield for longer.
Rates Borrower behavior Name Effect on the MBS investor
Fall Refinance; prepayments speed up Prepayment risk Principal early; reinvest lower
Rise Keep the old mortgage; prepayments slow Extension risk Stuck in a lower yield for longer
  • Neither outcome favors the MBS investor. Prepayment is a specialized form of reinvestment risk.

Currency Risk (Exchange Rate Risk)

  • Currency risk (exchange-rate risk): FX moves reduce the dollar value of a foreign investment. A strong local result can disappear on conversion to dollars.
  • Stronger dollar → foreign holdings are worth fewer dollars. Weaker dollar → they are worth more dollars.
Dollar Effect on foreign investments for a U.S. investor
Strengthens Dollar value falls
Weakens Dollar value rises
  • Example: a European fund gains 8% in euros and the euro falls 5% vs. the dollar. The dollar return is closer to 3%.
  • Who carries it: ADRs (foreign-currency dividends converted to dollars), international mutual funds and ETFs, foreign bonds (interest and principal), emerging markets (less stable currencies, so higher currency risk).
  • PRIME puts exchange-rate risk in the systematic group.

Capital Risk

  • Capital risk (principal risk): lose part or all of what you invested. Nearly every security has some. The degree changes.
Investment Capital-risk level Why
FDIC-insured bank deposits, up to $250,000 per depositor, per institution None Government guarantee
U.S. Treasuries None if held to maturity Full faith and credit. Sell early after rates rise and the price can be below cost (that loss is interest-rate risk)
Investment-grade bonds Low to moderate Default is possible and uncommon
Equities Moderate to high Price can fall to zero; no promised return of principal
Options Very high Can expire worthless: 100% loss of the premium
  • Equities carry more capital risk than high-quality bonds because prices swing and principal is not promised.
  • Diversification and asset allocation reduce capital risk. They do not eliminate it.
  • The positions with no capital risk in this lesson: FDIC deposits inside the $250,000 limit, and Treasuries held to maturity.

Credit Risk (Default Risk)

  • Credit risk (default risk): the issuer fails to pay scheduled interest or principal. This is a bond risk. Stockholders face business risk and market risk, not a missed coupon.
  • Higher credit risk → investors demand a higher yield.
  • Ratings: Moody’s, S&P, and Fitch. Scale runs from AAA / Aaa down to default: D at S&P and Fitch, C at Moody’s.
  • Investment grade = BBB− / Baa3 or above.
  • High-yield (junk) = below BBB− / Baa3. The line is not BBB+ / Baa1. Many institutions may hold investment grade only.

U.S. Treasuries None Full faith and credit
Agency: GNMA (Government National Mortgage Association) None Explicit U.S. guarantee
Agency: Fannie Mae / Freddie Mac Very low Implied backing, not an explicit guarantee
Investment-grade corporates Low to moderate Issuer’s finances

Liquidity Risk

  • Cannot sell quickly without a meaningful price cut. Sale can take weeks or months, or require a steep discount. Central issue for alternatives.
Highly liquid Illiquid
U.S. Treasuries DPPs
Large-cap stocks Non-traded REITs
ETFs Hedge funds

Money-market instruments Restricted securities
  • Open-end mutual funds are among the most liquid holdings: redeem with the fund at daily NAV on any business day.
  • Closed-end funds trade on an exchange. Liquidity depends on volume, like a stock.
  • DPPs, hedge funds, and non-traded REITs: lock-ups or limited redemption windows. On a “which is least liquid?” question, the alternative is usually the answer.
  • Restricted securities (unregistered stock or control stock) cannot be sold freely until SEC holding-period and volume conditions are met. This lesson does not state the day count.

Non-Systematic Risk (Unsystematic Risk)

  • Non-systematic = unsystematic = diversifiable = business = company-specific.
  • Tied to one company, industry, or sector. Diversification can reduce it or largely eliminate it.
  • Examples: unexpected CEO resignation, product recall, strike, a large lawsuit, a regulation aimed at one industry (energy rules are the lesson’s example).
  • An industry-specific rule is non-systematic. A recession or a Fed rate change is systematic.
  • About 25–30 stocks spread across sectors can virtually eliminate non-systematic risk. What remains is systematic risk.
  • “Diversifiable risk” on the exam means this risk.
  • Diversification = many issuers with low correlation, so one failure does not sink the book.
  • Asset allocation = the mix of asset classes (stocks, bonds, cash, alternatives). A book of 200 stocks can be fully diversified and still carry full stock-market systematic risk. Moving some of that money into bonds or cash is what cuts the market exposure.

Political Risk

  • Government action, instability, or policy change that hurts returns. Range: slow policy shifts through revolution or war.
  • Can hit an entire market or one industry, so it sits on both sides of the systematic line.
  • Forms: change of leadership or policy, new taxes or rules on an industry, nationalization, tariffs / trade restrictions / sanctions, war / revolution / civil unrest, currency controls (investors cannot take money out).
  • Highest exposure: international investments, especially emerging markets (less stable governments, weaker investor protections).
  • Vehicles: ADRs (home-country politics), international funds and ETFs, foreign bonds (default or capital controls).
  • Domestic investments are included. A new rule for healthcare, energy, or financials is political risk and, because it hits one sector, non-systematic risk.
  • Sovereign risk is narrower: a foreign government defaults on its debt. That is credit risk applied to government bonds. Nationalization, war, and regulatory change are political risk. They are not sovereign risk.

Diversification

  • Spread holdings across asset classes, sectors, industries, and geographies so one bad result is a small piece of the portfolio.
  • It is the tool for non-systematic risk. It does not remove systematic risk.
  • Benefit depends on correlation, not on ticker count. 50 tech stocks are one bet.
Correlation Movement Diversification benefit
+1.0 Lockstep None
0 No linear relationship Moderate
−1.0 Opposite directions Maximum
  • Asset allocation divides the portfolio among stocks, bonds, cash, real estate, commodities, and similar classes. Classes respond differently to the same economy, so allocation (with hedging) is how systematic stock-market exposure is managed.
  • Index puts are the hedge for a market-wide decline in a stock portfolio. Diversification will not do that job.
Diversification… Detail
Reduces non-systematic risk One company’s event hits a small slice
Leaves systematic risk in place A broad decline hits the book
Needs low correlation Same-sector piles do not count
Improves as variety grows Cross-sector, cross-country, cross-asset-class

Portfolio Rebalancing

  • Periodically put the portfolio back to its target asset allocation after returns cause drift.
  • It maintains the chosen risk level. It does not add a new layer of risk reduction. It is maintenance, not elimination.
  • Triggers are periodic: a calendar or a threshold. It is not a response to every daily tick.

Worked drift, target 60% stocks / 40% bonds:

Point Stocks Bonds Meaning
Start 60% 40% On target
After a bull market 70% 30% Overweight stocks = more risk than the investor chose
After rebalancing 60% 40% Back on target
  • A bull market without a rebalance quietly turns a conservative mix into a more aggressive one.
  • Mechanics: sell overweight (recent winners) and buy underweight (recent laggards). That is a built-in buy-low, sell-high discipline and it limits concentration in one class.
Method Trigger Advantage
Calendar-based Set schedule (quarterly, annually) Simple and consistent
Threshold-based Any sleeve drifts past a set band (lesson example: 5%) Reacts when the market actually moves the mix
  • The exam does not prefer one method. The point is returning to the target.

Hedging

  • Use an instrument to offset losses on a position you already have.
  • Unlike adding stocks, hedging can reduce systematic risk. The cost (usually an option premium) reduces return.
Strategy Mechanics Risk reduced Tradeoff
Protective put Buy a put on stock you own Downside is limited Premium reduces return; upside stays open minus the premium
Covered call Sell a call on stock you own Only a small cushion, equal to the premium Upside capped above the strike. This is not real downside protection
Index puts Buy puts on an index such as the S&P 500 Systematic risk of a diversified stock portfolio Premium cost

Protective put (lesson numbers)

  • Own 100 shares at $50. Buy a put with a $45 strike.
  • The put gains value once the stock is below $45.
  • Unprotected drop = $50 − $45 = $5 per share. The put has no intrinsic value inside that gap.
  • Maximum loss = that unprotected drop plus the premium. Upside stays unlimited minus the premium.
  • “Protective” means a long (purchased) put, so the investor pays premium up front.
  • A put bought with no stock (naked long put) has a different max loss: the premium only, because it can expire worthless. Once the stock is owned, the unprotected drop from price down to strike is added on top of the premium.

Covered call (lesson numbers)

  • Own 100 shares at $50. Sell a call with a $55 strike and collect premium.
  • Stock stays below $55: keep the premium as income.
  • Stock rises through $55: shares can be called away at $55. Further upside is gone.
  • The premium cushions only a small decline. For a defined floor, use the protective put.
  • Protective put: max loss defined, upside open. Covered call: upside capped, downside only lightly cushioned. Those profiles are opposites.

Three mitigation tools

Tool Handles Does not handle
Diversification Non-systematic risk Systematic market risk
Rebalancing Drift back to the target mix It maintains risk; it does not create new risk reduction
Hedging Systematic and position-level risk (index puts for the market; protective puts for a holding) The premium still comes out of return
Asset allocation Systematic exposure, by changing the asset-class mix It is not the same act as owning more stocks
  • Hierarchy: diversification for company risk, rebalancing for drift, hedging (and asset allocation) for market risk.

Exam traps

  • “Risk-free” still leaves Treasuries with inflation risk and interest-rate risk. Credit risk is the one they do not have. Capital risk is absent only if they are held to maturity.
  • Systematic = market = non-diversifiable. Non-systematic = unsystematic = diversifiable = business = company-specific. A synonym swap is the question.
  • PRIME risks are all systematic. Diversification does not clear them. The risk left in a diversified portfolio is systematic.
  • Beta of the market is 1.0, not 0. Above 1 = more volatile; below 1 = less volatile; 0 = uncorrelated.
  • Fed rate changes are systematic. A rule aimed at one industry is non-systematic (and can still be called political risk).
  • About 25–30 diversified stocks can largely remove non-systematic risk. 50 stocks in one sector, or 200 stocks with no other asset class, do not remove market risk.
  • Correlation +1.0 gives no diversification benefit. −1.0 gives the maximum. Low correlation is the requirement.
  • Asset allocation and diversification are different tools. Allocation (and hedging) addresses systematic risk.
  • Investment grade vs. junk splits at BBB− / Baa3, not BBB+ / Baa1.
  • Credit risk is a bond concept. The equity distractor is business risk and market risk.
  • ETNs are unsecured bank debt and carry issuer credit risk. ETFs hold securities in a trust and do not carry that sponsor credit risk.
  • GNMA is explicitly guaranteed (no credit risk). Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are implied backing (very low, not the same as GNMA).
  • GO bonds: taxing power. Revenue bonds: project revenue only, so more credit risk. Junk is high credit risk.
  • A downgrade hits price, not the coupon.
  • Rates up, bond prices down. Rates down, bond prices up. This is among the most-tested facts.
  • Zeros: maximum interest-rate risk and zero reinvestment risk. “No coupon means less to lose” is backwards.
  • Longer maturity + lower coupon = more rate sensitivity. The 30-year zero is the most rate-sensitive bond in the lesson.
  • Duration of 7 ≈ a 7% price drop if rates rise 1%.
  • Interest-rate risk and reinvestment risk move in opposite directions. Callables have the highest reinvestment risk.
  • MBS: rates fall = prepayment; rates rise = extension. Both are bad for the investor.
  • Strong dollar hurts a U.S. investor’s foreign holdings. Weak dollar helps. ADRs, foreign bonds, and emerging markets carry currency risk. Worked shrink: 8% local gain and a 5% currency loss → about a 3% dollar result.
  • Real return = nominal minus inflation (3% coupon, 4% inflation = a loss of purchasing power). Cash and long fixed-rate bonds are most exposed. TIPS adjust principal semiannually with CPI.
  • Most illiquid choice is usually a DPP, non-traded REIT, or hedge fund. Open-end funds are among the most liquid (daily NAV). Closed-end liquidity depends on trading volume.
  • Rebalancing maintains risk. After a bull market a 60/40 mix can drift to 70/30 and become riskier until it is sold back to target. You sell winners and buy laggards. Calendar and threshold (example band 5%) are both valid.
  • More stocks do not hedge a market crash. Index puts do. A protective put (long put on stock you own) defines max loss and leaves upside open. A covered call caps upside and is not true downside protection.
  • Protective-put gap: stock $50, strike $45, unprotected drop $5, then add the premium. A naked long put’s max loss is the premium alone.
  • Sovereign risk = foreign government default. It is not a synonym for political risk. Political risk also includes domestic industry regulation, nationalization, currency controls, and war.
  • FDIC capital-risk shield: $250,000 per depositor, per institution. Options can lose 100% of premium.

Orders and Strategies

Types of Orders

An order is the customer’s instruction to buy or sell under set conditions.

Order What happens Certainty Risk
Market Fills now at the best available price Execution is immediate No price control
Limit Fills only at the limit price or better Price is protected May never fill
Stop (stop-loss) Once the stop price prints, it becomes a market order Then fills at the next price Fill can be far from the stop
Stop-limit Once the stop prints, it becomes a limit order Then only at the limit or better May never fill if price gaps through the limit
  • Market = execute now, any price. Limit = my price or better. Stop = if price hits X, then market. Stop-limit = if price hits X, then only at Y or better.
  • A stop-loss is a plain stop, not a stop-limit. Adding a limit price is what creates a stop-limit, which can fail to execute.

Bid, Ask, and Spread

Term Who Meaning
Bid Buyer / dealer Highest price a buyer will pay
Ask (offer) Seller / dealer Lowest price a seller will accept
Spread Market Ask minus bid. Dealer’s profit opportunity and a liquidity signal
  • The customer buys at the ask (higher) and sells at the bid (lower). The customer always gets the worse price. The spread is the cost of immediacy.
  • Ask is always above the bid. They are never the same dollar amount. Example: $48.50 bid / $49.00 ask means the buyer pays $49.00 and the seller receives $48.50.
  • Narrow spread = high liquidity and a lower transaction cost (a large-cap name can be $0.01). Wide spread = thin trading and a higher cost (a small-cap can be $0.50 or more).

Trade Capacity

Capacity Role Pay Confirmation
Principal (dealer) Firm is the counterparty, usually from its own inventory Markup when selling to the customer; markdown when buying from the customer Disclose capacity. Markup is inside the price, not a separate line like a commission
Agency (broker) Firm matches the customer with another party and is not a counterparty Commission, disclosed separately Disclose capacity. Commission is a separate line
  • Commission = agency = broker. Markup or markdown = principal = dealer. The pay type reveals the capacity.
  • Best execution is owed in both capacities. It covers any transaction for or with a customer, agency or principal.
  • Interpositioning: placing a third party between the firm and the best market so the customer gets a worse price. That violates best execution.
  • Riskless principal: the dealer gets the customer order, buys in the market for its own account, and immediately resells to the customer. Almost no market risk, but it is still a principal trade and must be disclosed as principal. Compensation is a markup or a commission equivalent.
  • Dual capacity (principal and agent on the same trade) needs full disclosure and the customer’s consent. Otherwise the firm would profit from both sides.

Discretionary vs. Non-Discretionary Orders

Discretion exists when the rep chooses any one of the three A’s without prior approval for that trade:

  1. Asset (which security)
  2. Action (buy or sell)
  3. Amount (how many shares or how much money)
Rep decides Discretionary? Example
Asset Yes “Buy 100 shares of a tech company”
Action Yes “Manage my 100 Apple shares as you think best”
Amount Yes “Buy some Microsoft” / “Invest $10,000 in something good”
Time only, price only, or both No “Buy 100 AAPL when the price is right”
  • Non-discretionary: the customer already set asset, action, and amount. The rep only executes.
  • Before the first discretionary trade: written trading authorization or power of attorney. A verbal okay is not enough.
  • A principal must approve each discretionary trade promptly. Heightened supervision looks for churning (excess trading to generate commissions).
  • Time-and-price exception: picking only when or at what price is not discretion, and only if asset, action, and amount are already set. It dies at the end of that business day. Waiting until the next day makes the trade unauthorized.
  • “Buy something in the tech sector” is discretionary (the rep picks the name). “Buy 200 shares of Apple at the best price” is not.

  • “The customer asked for it” is not a shield. Unsolicited orders must still be accepted in good faith. A pattern that is clearly unsuitable or looks like manipulation must be flagged.

  • A stop does not guarantee the fill price. Once triggered it is a market order. A stop-limit can miss the trade entirely.
  • If no time-in-force is named, the order is a day order.
  • FOK is all-or-nothing right now. AON is all-or-nothing but can wait. IOC can partial-fill.
  • Buy limits and sell stops sit below the market. Sell limits and buy stops sit above. Students reverse this.
  • “Buy 100 Apple when the price is right” is not discretionary. “Invest $10,000 in something good” is. Time-and-price discretion dies at the close of that day.
  • Written authority must exist before the first discretionary trade, and a principal approves each discretionary trade promptly.
  • Solicited vs unsolicited follows the recommendation, not who entered the order. Mismarking the ticket is a violation by itself.
  • For a retail customer, a solicited recommendation triggers Reg BI. The FINRA suitability rule does not stack on top of recommendations Reg BI already covers.
  • Customers buy the ask and sell the bid. Commission means agent; markup or markdown means principal. Riskless principal is still disclosed as principal. Dual capacity needs disclosure and consent.
  • You cannot short in a cash account. Short stock and naked calls have unlimited loss. A covered call is not bearish. Selling a put is bullish.

Components of Return

Return of capital (return of principal) is a distribution that is not from earnings. Common in REITs, MLPs, and some mutual funds.

  • Not taxable when received. It reduces cost basis.
  • Example: invest $10,000, receive $1,000 return of capital, pay no tax, basis falls to $9,000. A later sale at $10,000 produces a $1,000 capital gain.
  • Once basis hits zero, further return of capital is taxable as a capital gain.
  • A distribution in excess of earnings and profits is return of capital.

Under T+1:

  • For a regular dividend, the ex-date and the record date are the same business day. They are not the same concept. Ex-date is a trading right (exchange or FINRA). Record date is the company’s ownership snapshot.
  • Buy before the ex-date and you get the dividend (the trade settles on the record date). Buy on or after the ex-date and you do not (settlement is after the record date).
  • On the ex-date the price usually opens lower by about the dividend. A $50 stock with a $1 dividend typically opens near $49. Neither the company nor the exchange sets that price by rule. An entitled holder is not “losing” the drop; they receive the cash.
  • Exception: a distribution worth 25% or more of the stock’s value has its ex-date on the first business day after the payable date.

Mutual funds are not stocks

  • The fund’s board sets the ex-date, not an exchange or FINRA.
  • The ex-date is usually one business day after the record date. Order is D-R-E-P. NAV drops by the distribution on the ex-date.

Worked stock timeline: declared January 15 ($0.50/share); ex-date and record date February 13; payable March 1. Buy February 12 and you are paid (settles February 13). Buy February 13 and you are not (settles February 14).

Yields, Total Return, and Basis Points

  • Current yield = annual income / current market price. Bonds: annual coupon / price. Stocks: annual dividend / price. Income only. Ignores capital gain or loss. Moves opposite price.
  • Bond: $50 coupon / $900 price = 5.56%.
  • Stock: $3.00 dividend / $75 price = 4.0%.
  • Yield to maturity (YTM) = annualized total if held to maturity: coupons, plus gain or loss versus par, plus time left.
  • Yield to call (YTC) = same idea if called at the earliest call date. Issuers call when rates fall so they can refinance cheaper.
  • Total dollar return = income + capital gain or loss. Total return % = that dollar return / initial investment. This is the full performance measure.
  • $40 dividends + $100 gain = $140. On a $1,000 investment that is 14%.
Purchase Non-callable ranking Callable, called at par
Discount YTM > current yield > coupon YTC > YTM > current yield > coupon
Par YTM = current yield = coupon All four yields equal
Premium Coupon > current yield > YTM Coupon > current yield > YTM > YTC
  • Discount: you paid under par, so maturity (or an earlier call) adds a capital gain. On a discount, the gain arrives sooner if called, so YTC > YTM.
  • Premium: you paid over par, so you give the premium back. If called, you give it back sooner, so YTC is the lowest yield.
  • Exam quote rule: use the lower of YTM and YTC (the worst case). That is usually YTC on a premium bond and YTM on a discount bond.

Exam traps

  • Unsold appreciation is unrealized. Current yield is the wrong answer when the question wants total performance.
  • Cash dividends are taxable. Qualified is not tax-free. Stock dividends are not taxable and do not change total basis. Return of capital is not income; it cuts basis, and after zero it is a capital gain.
  • Stock order is D-E-R-P. Mutual funds are D-R-E-P, set by the fund board, ex-date one business day after the record date.
  • Under T+1, a regular stock’s ex-date and record date are the same business day, but buying on the ex-date misses the dividend. The company does not set the ex-date.
  • A distribution of 25% or more of value uses the ex-date after the payable date.
  • Discount non-callable: YTM is the highest yield. Callable discount called at par: YTC is the highest. Premium: coupon is the highest; if callable, YTC is the lowest. Quote the lower of YTM and YTC.
  • 1 bp = 0.01%. 100 bps = 1%. Price and yield move opposite.
  • FIFO is the IRS default. Splits and stock dividends do not change total basis. Reinvested dividends raise total basis.
  • The DJIA is price-weighted. A split reduces a price-weighted name’s influence and does not change a market-cap weight. Russell 2000 is small-cap. You cannot invest in an index itself.

Trade Settlement and Corporate Actions

Settlement Cycles

  • Trade date (T) = execution. Settlement date = securities and payment actually move, and legal ownership changes. Between the two dates the trade is pending: the buyer has a right to the security but does not yet legally own it.
  • Dividends, interest, and voting rights follow who is the owner on the record date, which depends on settlement timing.
  • Count business days only. Weekends and exchange holidays do not count. Monday trades settle Tuesday. Friday trades settle Monday. A trade the day before a holiday skips the holiday.
Security Settlement Why
Stocks T+1 SEC standard cycle (shortened from T+2) for equities and corporate bonds
Corporate bonds T+1 Same SEC rule
Municipal bonds T+1 Exempt from the SEC standard-cycle rule; MSRB uniform practice
U.S. government securities (Treasuries and agencies) T+1 Exempt from the SEC rule; market convention
Listed options T+1 OCC rules, not the SEC settlement rule
Mutual funds T+1 NAV pricing; some settle the same day
New-issue T-bills at auction Issue date Typically 2–4 business days after the auction
Cash trade T+0 (same day) Only by special agreement. Not the default
  • Regular way = T+1, and it is automatic. Cash settlement must be requested. If the question says “regular way,” the answer is T+1, not same-day, and not the old T+2.
  • A shorter cycle cuts credit, market, and liquidity risk on unsettled trades.

Do not mix this up with payment. Settlement is T+1. The Regulation T payment deadline in a cash account is the settlement cycle plus two business days, so T+3. That rule is in Customer Accounts.

Total market value does not change. A forward split is not a taxable event and does not create income. Shares and price move in opposite directions by the same factor (the ratio number greater than 1). Total cost basis stays put; per-share basis changes.

Adjustments to Options and Open Orders

The OCC, not the broker-dealer, adjusts listed options for splits and stock dividends. Total notional value stays the same.

  • Forward split: contract count rises in proportion; strike falls in proportion.
  • 2-for-1 example: 1 call, $80 strike, 100 shares becomes 2 calls, $40 strike, each on 100 shares. Exposure is 200 × $40 = $8,000, same as before.
  • Another 2-for-1: a $50 strike call on 100 shares becomes two $25 calls on 100 shares each.
  • Options are adjusted on a reverse split as well.

Cash Accounts

  • The customer pays 100%. No borrowing from the broker-dealer. Open to anyone; no special approval.
  • Regulation T (Federal Reserve Board) requires full payment within one payment period: the settlement cycle plus two business days. With T+1 settlement, that deadline is T+3.
  • A firm may demand cash sooner (even by settlement) as house policy. That earlier demand is not the Regulation T deadline.
  • If the account is still unpaid at the end of the payment period, the firm must promptly cancel or liquidate the purchase, unless it has an extension from its examining authority. The lesson does not state how long that extension lasts.

Freeriding: buy, then sell, before paying for the buy. The T+3 gap is what makes it possible (buy Monday, sell before payment is due, and use the sale to fund the purchase). It violates Regulation T because the customer profits on securities never paid for.

  • Penalty: a 90-day freeze. The account is not closed. The customer may still trade, but any new buy needs settled funds already in the account. Unsettled sale proceeds cannot be used as credit.
  • Freeriding is not day trading. The buy and the sell can be days apart. The defining act is selling before paying.

AML Compliance

Definition of money laundering

  • Money laundering disguises illegally obtained money so it looks legitimate. The cash is real. This is not counterfeiting.
  • Typical sources: drug trafficking, fraud, terrorism financing, tax evasion, embezzlement.
  • Securities firms are targets because of large volume, complex instruments (derivatives, shells, offshore accounts), and fast electronic transfers.
  • Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) (1970): recordkeeping and reports. Applies to all financial institutions, not only banks. Sometimes called the Anti-Money Laundering Act. Still in force.
  • USA PATRIOT Act (2001): built on the BSA after 9/11. Added customer identification, information sharing, and enhanced due diligence. Did not replace the BSA.
  • Every FINRA member must have a written AML program.

Three stages of money laundering

Stage Name What happens Typical technique
1 Placement Illegal cash enters the system. Most vulnerable (physical cash). Structuring / smurfing: break cash into deposits under $10,000 to dodge a CTR. Also money orders, cashier's checks, mixing with a legit business, smuggling cash abroad.
2 Layering Complex trades separate money from its source. Most complex to trace. Multiple accounts, shells, foreign wires, converting cash into stocks, bonds, or derivatives. Rapid buy-and-sell across accounts is layering, not integration.
3 Integration "Clean" money re-enters the economy. Hardest to detect. Real estate, businesses, luxury goods, investments, using funds as loan collateral.
  • Structuring is placement, not layering. It is a crime even if the funds are legitimate. A deposit under $10,000 is structuring only if the purpose is to evade the CTR.
  • Laundry memory: put dirty clothes in (placement), run the cycles (layering), take them out clean (integration).

AML compliance program

Five required parts (order on the exam can change; do not swap in a fake sixth):

  1. Policies and procedures reasonably designed to detect and cause reporting of suspicious activity.
  2. AML Compliance Officer (AMLCO): day-to-day owner. Need not be senior management. Must be identified to FINRA; notify FINRA promptly of a change.
  3. Ongoing training of relevant personnel.
  4. Independent testing each calendar year. Internal audit staff qualify if they do not run the AML program. Outside testers also qualify. Independence means separate from the people who administer the program, not "must be outside the firm."
  5. Customer due diligence (CDD): nature and purpose of the relationship, beneficial owners of legal-entity customers, ongoing monitoring.
  • Program must be approved by senior management.
  • Two-calendar-year testing exception only if all three are true: no customer transactions, no customer accounts, and no introducing-broker activity for customer accounts.
  • Must include a Customer Identification Program (CIP) under the USA PATRIOT Act.

CIP — four items, generally collected before opening; identity verified within a reasonable time before or after opening:

  • Name (full legal name)
  • Date of birth (individuals)
  • Address (residential or business)
  • Identification number: TIN for U.S. persons; passport number and country of issuance (or other permitted alternative) for non-U.S. persons

Also: keep verification records; screen names against government terrorist lists; give the customer notice that identity is being verified. Documentary (license, passport), non-documentary (credit report, database), or both. A photo ID is not always required. A pending TIN is allowed if the application was filed before opening and the number is obtained within a reasonable period. CIP applies to every new account, including an existing customer opening another account.

Flow: CIP builds the identity file at onboarding → ongoing AML monitoring → suspicion → SAR / CTR to FinCEN. Collection and verification are separate checkpoints. Monitoring can start while verification is still open.

Suspicious Activity Report (SAR)

  • File a SAR with FinCEN when the firm knows, suspects, or has reason to suspect a transaction (or related aggregate) of $5,000 or more that:
  • involves illegal-activity funds, or
  • is designed to evade reporting (structuring), or
  • has no apparent lawful purpose, or
  • is meant to facilitate crime.
  • Proof is not required. Reasonable suspicion is enough. Structuring is a listed trigger, not merely a red flag. Related deposits are aggregated (three $4,900 deposits = $14,700).
  • CTR is cash only. A suspicious wire, check, or securities trade above $5,000 is a SAR, not a CTR, even if it is over $10,000.
  • Chain: RR reports to the AMLCO → AMLCO decides → the firm files with FinCEN. The RR does not file with FinCEN, the FBI, the SEC, or the police.
  • Deadlines from initial detection (internal review does not pause the clock; never later than 60 days):
  • Suspect identified: 30 calendar days
  • No suspect: 60 calendar days
  • Filing is mandatory when the criteria are met. Filing does not require closing the account.
  • Tipping off is a federal violation. No director, officer, employee, or agent may tell the customer a SAR exists or was filed, even if asked. Who may know: FinCEN, federal/state/local law enforcement, federal regulators examining the firm, and SROs (FINRA) examining SAR compliance.
  • A routine source-of-funds question is not tipping off. The RR still reports first. Any customer follow-up is the AMLCO's call.

Currency Transaction Report (CTR)

  • File a CTR with FinCEN for cash transactions exceeding $10,000 in one business day. Deposits, withdrawals, currency exchanges, and other cash payments or transfers. No suspicion required. Automatic.
  • Deadline: 15 calendar days after the transaction.
  • Same person, same day: aggregate transactions the firm knows are by or for that person. Cash in and cash out are totaled separately. An $8,000 cash deposit plus a $4,000 cash withdrawal is not $12,000. Each side is tested against $10,000 on its own.
  • The customer may know a CTR was filed. There is no tipping-off ban for a CTR.
  • Keep CTR records 5 years from the report date.
  • Structuring / smurfing: deliberately splitting cash to stay under $10,000 (example: $9,500 Monday and $9,500 Tuesday). Illegal even if the money is clean. The firm files a SAR, not only a CTR.
  • Memory: CTR = Cash over $10,000 (automatic). SAR = Suspicious at $5,000 or more.

FinCEN

  • FinCEN (Financial Crimes Enforcement Network) is a bureau of the U.S. Treasury. It administers the BSA, writes the rules, and receives SARs and CTRs.
  • It analyzes data and shares intelligence with law enforcement (FBI, DEA, IRS Criminal Investigation). It does not investigate crimes and is not part of Justice or the SEC. It can impose civil penalties for BSA violations.
  • Chain: firm files → FinCEN analyzes → law enforcement investigates.

OFAC and the SDN List

  • OFAC (Office of Foreign Assets Control) is also Treasury, but it enforces sanctions, not BSA reporting. Authority: presidential orders and statutes, including the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA). Not the BSA.
  • Targets: sanctioned countries and regimes, terrorists, narcotics traffickers, WMD proliferators.
  • SDN List (Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons): U.S. persons generally cannot deal with listed parties. Assets must be blocked (frozen). List is public and updated.
  • Three broker-dealer duties: screen (new accounts and transactions, and on an ongoing basis) → block immediately (no trade, withdrawal, or move) → report the block to OFAC within 10 business days.
  • Civil OFAC violations are strict liability. Intent is not required. Penalties can be severe, civil and criminal.
  • OFAC compliance is separate from the AML program. A firm needs both. One transaction can be blocked and trigger a SAR.

Exam traps

  • AMLCO need not be senior management. The program must be approved by senior management.
  • Independent testing can be internal. Testers must be outside the AML program, not necessarily outside the firm. The 2-year cycle needs all three absences.
  • CIP is four items only. It covers every new account, including additional accounts for existing customers. Collect before opening; verify within a reasonable time before or after.
  • $5,000 (SAR) vs over $10,000 cash (CTR). A "10 days" SAR answer is the OFAC window, and that window is business days.
  • Cash-in and cash-out are not added together for a CTR.
  • Structuring is illegal with clean money, is placement, and requires a SAR.
  • RR reports to the AMLCO. The AMLCO files. Never tip off. Filing a SAR does not force the account closed. A source-of-funds question is not tipping off.
  • BSA (1970) came first and still applies to non-banks. USA PATRIOT Act (2001) did not replace it.
  • FinCEN does not investigate. SARs and CTRs go to FinCEN, not straight to the FBI.
  • Mandatory sharing: authorities ask, firms must answer (ordinarily 14 days), and the request stays secret. Voluntary sharing: firms opt in with a FinCEN notice and get a privacy safe harbor.
  • OFAC: block first, then report within 10 business days. Screening is ongoing. Strict liability. Comply with OFAC and FinCEN both.

Trade confirmation (the receipt) goes out at or before completion of the transaction (settlement), not on trade date. Include:

  • Date and time, security name and CUSIP, quantity, price and total, settlement date
  • Capacity: agent (disclose commission) or principal (disclose markup/markdown)
  • Every order ticket is marked solicited (firm recommended) or unsolicited (customer's idea). That mark drives suitability / Reg BI.

Holding customer mail

All four must be true:

  • Written instructions (a phone call is not enough)
  • A stated time period
  • Hold does not exceed 3 months, unless an acceptable reason is given
  • The firm can still reach the customer another way (email, website)

Acceptable reasons to go past 3 months: extended travel, safety or security. Convenience is not acceptable. "For convenience" caps the hold at 3 months no matter how long the customer asks.

During the hold the firm must: tell the customer in writing about alternate ways to monitor the account and give required disclosures (including SIPC information); get confirmation the customer received that notice; re-check at reasonable intervals that the instructions still apply; stop tampering, unauthorized holds, or misuse by an associated person.

Business continuity plans

  • Every member needs a written BCP. No small-firm exemption. It must be reasonably designed so the firm can meet existing customer obligations.
  • Cover, to the extent they apply: data backup and recovery; alternate communications with customers, employees, and regulators; critical operations; financial and operational assessment; alternate physical location; customer access to funds and securities; regulatory reporting. If a category does not apply, document why it was left out.
  • Review at least annually, and update after a material change (operations, structure, business, or location — an office move counts).
  • Approver of the plan and of the annual review: senior management who is also a registered principal.
  • Disclose to customers in writing how the BCP addresses a future disruption.
  • Report two emergency contacts to FINRA. At least one must be senior management and a registered principal.

Customer protection and custody

Customer Protection Rule (SEC): stop the firm from financing itself with customer assets.

  • Possession or control of fully paid securities and excess margin securities. Excess margin = value above 140% of the customer's debit balance. Margin securities up to 140% of the debit may be pledged to finance the firm, but that collateral cannot be diverted to the firm's own trades. Segregation from proprietary activity still applies.
  • Special Reserve Bank Account: cash owed to customers, for their exclusive benefit. The firm may withdraw only an amount above the required deposit. Borrowing the required reserve is a violation.

Net Capital Rule: keep minimum net capital (liquid assets minus liabilities) at all times. Below the minimum, the firm must stop doing business until the shortfall is fixed. Customer Protection keeps customer money separate. Net capital keeps the firm solvent. Two different layers.

Improper use (FINRA): no exceptions. Using customer funds for the firm's expenses or trading losses violates both this ban and the Customer Protection Rule.

Commingling is an SEC hypothecation issue, not that FINRA improper-use rule:

  • Cannot pledge customer securities together with the firm's own under a lien for a firm loan, with or without consent.
  • Cannot mix one customer's pledged securities with another's unless each customer gives written consent.

SIPC, created by the Securities Investor Protection Act of 1970 (SIPA). Nonprofit funded by member broker-dealers, not the federal government. Not a government agency. Protects missing assets when a firm fails. Does not insure market losses.

Coverage Amount
Securities and cash combined, per customer $500,000
Cash sublimit inside that total $250,000
  • Example: $200,000 securities + $400,000 cash pays $450,000 ($200,000 plus the $250,000 cash cap). Cash sits inside the $500,000, not on top of it.
  • Covered: missing stocks, bonds, Treasuries, mutual funds, and other registered securities; cash in the brokerage account to buy securities.
  • Not covered: market-value declines, commodity futures, forex, fixed annuities that are not registered securities, bad advice.
  • Separate capacity: individual, joint, and IRA are each a separate customer, each up to $500,000.
  • Suitcase memory: the bag holds $500,000; the cash pocket holds $250,000 (half) and still counts against the bag.

Privacy requirements (Regulation S-P)

  • Regulation S-P is the SEC privacy rule implementing the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA). It covers broker-dealers, registered investment advisers, and investment companies.
  • It does not cover banks, thrifts, or credit unions. Those follow a parallel GLBA rule from their banking regulator (OCC, Federal Reserve, FDIC, or NCUA).
  • NPI (nonpublic personal information): SSN, account numbers, transaction history, income, net worth, balances, and information from transactions with the customer. Public records and government filings generally are not NPI.
Duty Rule

  • An opt-out lasts indefinitely until the customer revokes it. No 2-year, 3-year, 6-year, or annual reset. The annual notice is not a new opt-out.
  • Sharing with affiliates does not require an opt-out.

Encrypted numbers the recipient cannot decode do not count. Exception: the firm's own agent marketing only the firm's products, if that agent cannot initiate charges to the account.

Counted over any 30-calendar-day window. The window is cumulative.

Best interest and suitability

Regulation Best Interest (Reg BI): SEC standard when a broker-dealer recommends to a retail customer. Act in the customer's best interest at the time of the recommendation. Four obligations:

Obligation What it requires
Disclosure Form CRS plus full and fair disclosure of material facts, including conflicts
Care Reasonable diligence, care, and skill
Conflict of interest Written policies to identify, disclose, and mitigate or eliminate conflicts
Compliance Written policies and procedures to comply with Reg BI

Form CRS (Customer Relationship Summary): services, fees, conflicts, disciplinary history, and where to get more information. Deliver at the beginning of the relationship, or before or at the recommendation. Cap is a maximum, not a required length: 2 pages for a standalone broker-dealer or adviser; up to 4 pages for a dual registrant combining both.

FINRA suitability applies only where Reg BI does not (example: recommendations to institutional customers). It does not also apply to a retail recommendation already covered by Reg BI. The two rules cover different customers. Three suitability duties:

Type Test
Reasonable-basis Suitable for at least some investors
Customer-specific Suitable for this customer's profile
Quantitative The series of trades is not excessive (anti-churning)

Know Your Customer (KYC): reasonable diligence to know essential facts about every customer, including self-directed accounts, so the firm can service the account, know who may act, and follow the law. Profile items: objectives, risk tolerance, income and net worth, tax status, time horizon, liquidity needs, other holdings.

A recommendation is a communication that reasonably looks like a suggestion to act (a specific security, a strategy, or an account type). Not a recommendation: general education, an answer to an unsolicited question about a specific security, or a research report with no call to action. An unsolicited trade triggers neither Reg BI nor suitability. The firm still executes it.

Exam traps

  • Complaints are 4 years, not 3 or 6. SARs are 5 years, not the 6-year FINRA default. Account records run 6 years after closure. Articles and charters last the life of the firm.
  • The 2-year access rule sits inside the longer period.
  • Electronic storage needs WORM or an audit trail.
  • Confirmations go out by settlement, and they show agent vs principal compensation. Solicited vs unsolicited is marked on the order ticket.
  • Quarterly is the FINRA minimum. Monthly is required for penny stocks, not for every active account. A price change is not activity. A deposit does not move the account off the quarterly cycle.
  • Mail hold: writing required; 3-month cap; convenience does not extend it.
  • BCP: no small-firm exemption; annual review and material-change updates; approver is senior management and a principal; two FINRA emergency contacts, at least one wearing both hats.
  • Excess margin is above 140% of the debit. The reserve account's required deposit is not the firm's money. Consent does not allow mixing customer securities with the firm's own under a firm-loan lien.
  • SIPC: $500,000 with a $250,000 cash sublimit inside it. Not market-loss insurance. Not government-funded. Separate capacities each get $500,000.
  • Regulation S-P is opt-out, never expires, and does not cover banks. Affiliates need no opt-out. Account numbers still cannot go to a non-affiliate for its marketing. Breach notice: no later than 30 days.
  • More than 25 in 30 calendar days is retail and needs pre-use principal approval. 25 or fewer is correspondence. Institutional still gets supervision. 13 + 13 = 26. New firms file for the first year. Options: 10 calendar days before first use. A later-distributed appearance recording can become retail.
  • Do-not-call: both lists; firm-specific lasts indefinitely and must be honored within 30 days; hours follow the customer; 18 months vs 3 months; no blocked caller ID.
  • Reg BI is retail recommendations only, four obligations, Form CRS 2 / 4 pages. Suitability does not stack on top of Reg BI for the same retail recommendation. KYC still applies to self-directed accounts. Unsolicited trades trigger neither standard.

Prohibited Activities

Why this order. Manipulation, then insider trading, then harm to a customer, then deals away from the firm.

Definition of market manipulation

  • Intentional conduct that artificially moves price or volume. Real supply and demand should set price.
  • To prove the broad SEC antifraud violation: (1) manipulation or deception, (2) materiality, (3) a connection to a purchase or sale, (4) scienter (intent to deceive).
Rule Reach
SEC antifraud rule Catch-all. Any fraud or deceit in connection with buying or selling securities. Covers manipulation and insider trading. Applies to anyone.
Securities Exchange Act of 1934 antimanipulation provision Narrower. Transactions that create a false appearance of active trading or artificially affect price.
FINRA antimanipulation rule Manipulative, deceptive, or fraudulent devices by FINRA members and their associated persons only.

Types of market manipulation

Scheme What it is
Market rumors False or misleading information to move the price
Pump and dump Hype a thin stock, sell into the inflated price, price collapses
Front running Trade ahead of a known pending customer order
Churning (excessive trading) Trades to generate commissions, not to help the customer
Marking the close Trades at or near the close to set the closing price
Marking the open Same idea at the open
Backing away A market maker refuses its published bid or offer at the quoted price and size
Freeriding Cash account: sell before paying. Regulation T payment is not due until T+3. Buying, watching a rise, and selling before payment uses the sale to fund the purchase.
Withholding Keeping hot-IPO shares for the firm or its employees instead of the public
Wash trading Same person is the true buyer and seller (often through accounts they control). Beneficial ownership does not change. Count of trades does not matter.
Matched orders Typically two or more parties secretly prearrange offsetting orders so it looks like ownership changed.
Painting the tape A series of trades (one person or a group) to fake genuine investor interest. The trader need not be both sides. The point is to induce others to trade.
Spoofing Enter bids or offers intending to cancel before execution, to fake buying or selling interest. Unlike marking the close, the orders are not meant to fill.

Spot-the-scheme:

If you see… It is…
Broker buys, then fills a large customer buy Front running
High commission-to-equity, in-and-out trades Churning
Thin stock, exaggerated promotion, then a sale Pump and dump
One person, own accounts, no ownership change Wash trading
Separate parties prearrange both sides Matched orders
Market maker will not trade at its quote Backing away

  • Churning needs all three: the rep controlled the account, trading was excessive for the customer's objectives, and the rep acted with intent to defraud or reckless disregard.
  • Front running is a pending customer order. Insider trading is MNPI from a corporate (or other duty) source. Both are illegal. They are not the same charge.
  • Freeriding is a cash-account Regulation T violation. Withholding is an IPO allocation abuse. An old NASD "Free-Riding and Withholding" interpretation covered hot-issue IPO abuses only. On the exam, keep the two terms separate.
  • Backing away is market makers only. Ordinary investors do not publish firm quotes.
  • Information-based schemes: rumors, pump and dump. Activity-based schemes: wash trades, marking the close.

Penalties for market manipulation

All three tracks can run at once.

Who What
SEC (civil) Disgorgement, injunctions, civil fines. Burden: preponderance of the evidence (more likely than not).
DOJ (criminal) Up to 20 years in prison. Individuals up to $5 million. Entities up to $25 million. Burden: beyond a reasonable doubt.
FINRA Fines, censure, suspension, bar (person), expulsion (firm). FINRA can bar with no criminal case.
  • Disgorgement = give back the profits. Injunction = court order to stop. Censure = formal reprimand on the permanent record. Bar is permanent (the person cannot work in securities again). Suspension is temporary. Expulsion ends the firm's FINRA membership.
  • A bar hits a person. Expulsion hits a member firm.
  • Criminal penalties under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 ($5 million / 20 years for an individual; $25 million for an entity) are the same numbers used for insider trading.

Insider trading

  • Buying or selling based on MNPI while breaching a fiduciary duty or other duty of trust and confidence. Being an insider who trades is legal. Trading on MNPI is not.
  • Insiders: officers, directors, shareholders who own more than 10% of a class of equity securities, and tippees.
  • Material: a reasonable investor would consider it important (merger, earnings surprise, major contract, dividend change, regulatory action). Also: would it likely move the price?
  • Nonpublic: not disseminated broadly enough for the market to absorb it. A press release is not instantly public. Study guideline, not a statute: still nonpublic if less than one full trading day has passed. Ten minutes is never enough. Courts decide case by case.
  • Same SEC antifraud rule as manipulation. The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 gives the SEC authority over manipulative and deceptive devices.
  • SEC civil penalty: up to 3x the profit gained or loss avoided (treble damages). Controlling persons (supervisors and firms) that fail to prevent it are also liable.
  • A duty of trust can come from a family or close personal relationship, an agreement to keep the information confidential, or a history of sharing confidences. A tippee need not work at the company. Merely overhearing strangers, with no duty in the chain, does not by itself create liability. Lawyers, accountants, and friends who trade on a tip can be liable.
  • Safe harbor: a written trading plan adopted in good faith before the person learns MNPI. Later trades that follow the plan are an affirmative defense. The plan cannot be changed to exploit inside information.
  • Tipper: the person who passes MNPI. Liable even without trading if they received a personal benefit and the person they tipped trades.
  • Tippee: liable if they knew or should have known the information was material and nonpublic and that the tipper breached a duty, and they trade (or tip someone who trades). "Should have known" is objective: what a reasonable person in that position would recognize. Claiming naivety fails when the context is obvious.
  • Firms must keep information barriers (ethical walls / Chinese walls) so MNPI does not flow from investment banking or M&A to trading and sales. The barrier is about information flow, not only physical walls. Federal law requires written supervisory procedures to prevent insider trading. A failed barrier can make the firm a controlling person.

Material nonpublic information

Both parts must be true. Nonpublic lunch plans are not material. Published earnings are material but public.

Becomes public only when both: widely disseminated through channels investors use (press release, SEC filing, major news) and the market has had time to absorb it. A release 5 minutes ago can still be nonpublic.

Examples of material items: unreleased earnings, mergers, tender offers, major contracts, patents, lawsuits, SEC investigations, CEO resignation, dividend changes, splits, buybacks, impending bankruptcy.

Identifying involved parties

Party Liability
Insider (officer, director, or more than 10% holder) Trades on MNPI while owing a duty to the company and shareholders
Tipper Passes MNPI, gets a personal benefit, and the tippee trades. Liable even if the tipper never trades.
Tippee Receives the tip and trades, knowing or having reason to know it was material and nonpublic. No trade (and no further tip that leads to a trade) means no tippee liability.
Controlling person Employer, supervisor, or firm that fails to keep adequate procedures. Liable without personally trading.

Improper use of customer securities or funds

Borrowing and lending both ways. A registered person generally cannot borrow from or lend to a customer. A customer includes anyone who has, or in the previous 6 months had, a securities account assigned to that person at any firm. Indirect loans through family or an outside business are covered the same way.

Any exception works only if the firm's written procedures allow it.

Exception Extra condition
Immediate family Procedures may waive notice and approval
Customer is a financial institution lending in the ordinary course Procedures may waive notice and approval only if the loan is on terms generally offered to the public
Pre-existing personal relationship Must have been formed before the broker-customer relationship. Written notice and written approval before the loan, and again before any change or extension. A friendship that starts after the account opens does not qualify.
Outside business relationship Bona fide and outside the broker-customer relationship. When it began is a factor, not a hard "must predate" rule. Same written notice and prior written approval.
Both are registered persons at the same member Same written notice and prior written approval

Sharing profits or losses is banned unless all three are true: the customer gives written authorization, the firm gives written authorization, and the split is proportionate to each person's contribution. Disproportionate sharing is allowed only if the customer is an immediate family member (example: the rep puts in 30% of the capital and takes 50% of the profits). One approval is not enough.

Custody: segregate customer securities and funds from firm assets. Do not use customer securities as collateral for the firm's own loans except as margin rules allow.

Trusted contact (customer-account information rule): the firm must make reasonable efforts to obtain one. The customer may refuse. The contact is a natural person age 18 or older, a resource for the firm, not a power of attorney, and cannot direct trades.

Activities of unregistered persons

Unregistered persons cannot:

  • Receive commissions or other transaction-based compensation
  • Solicit (recommend or suggest a securities transaction)
  • Take orders
  • Execute transactions

Clerical staff may describe products in general terms ("we offer mutual funds"), answer basic facts, and hand the customer to a registered representative. They cannot recommend, discuss suitability, or suggest a specific trade. Where the person works is not the violation. What they do is.

Continuing (trailing) commissions to a rep who retires (including for total disability) and leaves the industry are allowed if a bona fide contract was made while the rep was still registered. The contract must bar soliciting new business, opening new accounts, and servicing the accounts. Payments can continue from existing customers even if those customers later add money or securities (example: a share of 12b-1 fees). Without that pre-retirement contract, the payments do not qualify. This is not paying an unregistered person for new business.

Falsifying or withholding documents

  • Signature of convenience is prohibited. Oral permission does not authorize signing a customer's name. A written power of attorney lets the agent sign the agent's own name "as agent for" the customer. Covers applications, confirmations, and other documents.
  • Also violations: predating or postdating tickets, falsifying customer information (net worth or objectives changed so a trade looks suitable — a separate charge from an unsuitable recommendation), false records, destroying records during the retention period (illegal even with no investigation), and destroying records to obstruct an inquiry (adds obstruction).
  • FINRA members and associated persons must cooperate with investigations (information, testimony, documents). Failure to respond can itself produce a bar. More than one-third of FINRA cases that end in an industry bar involve failure to cooperate. A suspension for noncompliance typically becomes a permanent bar after 90 days if the person still has not complied. Pleading the Fifth to a FINRA request is treated as a failure to cooperate.

Manipulative, deceptive, or fraudulent devices

SEC antifraud rule, in connection with a purchase or sale, bans three things:

  1. An untrue statement of a material fact (a bond called "risk-free" when it has credit risk).
  2. Omitting a material fact that makes other statements misleading (recommending a stock while hiding a large personal position that is a real conflict). Silence is a violation only when it makes what was said misleading. Not every personal holding must be disclosed.
  3. Any act or course of business that operates as a fraud (Ponzi scheme, systematic commission overcharging).
  • FINRA antimanipulation rule mirrors this for members and associated persons. If the question is about a FINRA member, that rule is the fit. If the question is securities fraud generally, use the SEC antifraud rule.
  • Narrower OTC rules cover brokers, dealers, and municipal securities dealers only, not "any person," and only OTC transactions:
  • SEC OTC antifraud rule: fraud in OTC transactions.
  • SEC non-member misrepresentation rule: false claims about the firm's registration or the merits of a security.
  • The broad SEC rule covers exchange-listed and OTC securities. If the question does not say OTC, use the broad rule.

IPO restrictions (new issue rule)

FINRA new issue rules stop restricted persons from buying IPO shares at the offering price so hot deals are not reserved for insiders. Restricted persons may buy in the aftermarket.

Restricted person Why
FINRA member firms and their associated persons Industry insiders
Certain immediate family of associated persons Stops a family workaround
Finders and fiduciaries of the managing underwriter People on the deal
Portfolio managers Anyone with authority to buy or sell for a bank, savings and loan, insurance company, investment company, investment adviser, or collective investment account
Owners of a broker-dealer Same insider problem as associated persons

Immediate family includes spouse, children, parents, siblings, and in-laws. Relationship type alone does not restrict them. Any one of these does:

  • Material support: more than 25% of income either way. A shared household is deemed material support by itself.
  • The associated person's firm, or an affiliate, sells the IPO shares to that family member.
  • The associated person controls the allocation.

So a mother-in-law in the same household is restricted. An adult sibling living alone, with no support tie, buying through another firm, with no allocation control by the rep, is not.

May still buy at the offering:

  • Investment companies (they buy for fund shareholders)
  • Certain employee benefit plans (they buy for participants)
  • De minimis: beneficial interests of restricted persons do not exceed 10% of the account in aggregate. An 8% restricted interest, with no other restricted holder, can receive shares.

Compliance: written representation that the buyer is not restricted, obtained within the 12 months before the sale (a representation from 13 months ago is stale). Keep records at least 3 years after the last new-issue sale to that account. The representation comes before the sale.

Exam traps

  • The SEC antifraud rule is the catch-all (manipulation and insider trading, any person). The Exchange Act antimanipulation provision is narrower. The FINRA rule is members only. OTC antifraud rules are brokers, dealers, and municipal securities dealers, and OTC only.
  • Front running ≠ insider trading. Wash trade (one beneficial owner) ≠ matched orders (separate cooperating parties) ≠ painting the tape (fake interest, any number of sides). Spoofing cancels; marking the close executes. Backing away is market makers only.
  • Churning needs control, excessiveness, and intent or reckless disregard.
  • Freeriding (Regulation T, T+3, cash account) ≠ withholding (hot IPO kept from the public).
  • Criminal: $5 million / 20 years individual, $25 million entity, for manipulation and for insider trading. Civil treble is extra. FINRA can bar with no DOJ case. Bar is permanent; censure is a reprimand; expulsion is the firm.
  • Being an insider is legal. More than 10% is the shareholder test. A tippee who does not trade is not liable. A tipper who never trades can be, if there is a personal benefit and someone trades. "Should have known" is objective. Overhearing strangers, with no duty, is not enough. Less than one full trading day is a study guideline, not a statute. A 5-minute-old release can still be nonpublic.
  • Disgorgement plus treble: $100,000 back plus up to $300,000 = up to $400,000. Controlling person: greater of $1 million or 3x, even with no personal trade. Contemporaneous-trader suits are private.
  • IPO restriction is the offering price only. Family is restricted by circumstances (more than 25% support, shared household, the rep's firm selling, or the rep allocating), not by the family label alone. De minimis is 10% (8% can still buy). Representation within 12 months before the sale. Records 3 years.
  • An omission violates only when it makes other statements misleading.
  • Borrowing runs both ways and looks back 6 months. Only the personal-relationship exception must predate the account. Family and ordinary-course bank loans may skip notice if procedures say so. Profit sharing needs both writings and a proportionate split unless the customer is immediate family (30% in / 50% out only for family).
  • Specified adult = 65+ or 18+ with an impairment. Holds are 15 + 10 = 25, then +30 = 55 business days if reported to the state. Trusted-contact notice within 2 business days. The contact is not a POA. The hold covers transactions, not only disbursements. Skip notice to the suspected exploiter.
  • Unregistered staff may describe products generally. They may not recommend or take orders. Trailing commissions need a contract signed while registered and bar new business, new accounts, and servicing.
  • Oral permission does not let you sign the customer's name. The Fifth is non-cooperation with FINRA. 90 days turns the suspension into a bar.
  • OBA = prior written notice, no supervision duty. Private securities with compensation = approve and supervise. No compensation = acknowledge only. Selling away is the missing notice (or missing approval), even if the customer makes money.

Registration and Continuing Education

Registered vs. non-registered persons

  • A registered person has passed the SIE plus a top-off exam, been registered with FINRA through a member firm, and had Form U4 filed by that firm.
  • A registered representative (for example Series 6 or Series 7) and a registered principal (for example Series 24) are both registered persons. Only a principal may supervise other representatives.
  • A non-registered person is affiliated with the firm and has no active registration. Work is limited to clerical and administrative tasks.

  • Casual talk about a specific stock with a customer is selling and requires registration.

SRO qualification and registration

  • Anyone associated with a member firm who engages in the securities business must register with FINRA. There is no self-registration. A firm sponsorship is required.

Four steps, usual order. Registration is effective only after both exams are passed. The SIE may be passed before or after hiring.

  1. Pass the SIE. Open to anyone 18 or older. No firm sponsor.
  2. Obtain sponsorship from a member firm. A top-off exam cannot be taken without it.
  3. The firm files Form U4. That filing also schedules the top-off exam.
  4. Pass the top-off exam for the business the person will do.

Form U4 (Uniform Application for Securities Industry Registration or Transfer):

  • Filed by the firm, electronically, through the CRD (Central Registration Depository). The individual does not self-file.
  • Disclosures include 10-year employment history and 5-year residential history.
  • Criminal: any felony charge or conviction, plus charges or convictions for listed misdemeanors (investment-related, fraud, wrongful taking of property, and similar categories on the form).
  • Also: regulatory actions, investigations, discipline, civil judgments and liens, and customer complaints.
  • Continuing duty: amend Form U4 within 30 days of learning facts that require an amendment. If the amendment involves a statutory disqualification, the deadline is 10 days after the disqualification occurs. The individual must keep the firm informed.

Background investigation, before the firm applies to register the person:

  • Reasonable investigation of good character, business reputation, qualifications, and experience.
  • Within 30 days of the Form U4 filing, verify that the form is accurate and complete, including a search of reasonably available public records.
  • Criminal check through fingerprints (FBI), credit history, reasons for leaving prior employers, and prior disclosure events.
  • There is no separate 3-year background-check lookback. The form itself carries the 10-year employment and 5-year residence history.

Fingerprinting:

  • The SEC fingerprint rule requires fingerprints of associated persons. FINRA runs registration; the SEC requires the prints. Prints go to the FBI.
  • If the firm does not submit fingerprints within 30 days after FINRA receives Form U4, the registration becomes inactive.
  • Limited exception only if the person does none of these: sell securities; access securities, funds, or original books and records; supervise anyone who does those things.

Statutory disqualification

  • Statutory disqualification makes a person ineligible to associate with a FINRA member. It is a status, and it is not a lifetime bar in every case. Some events age off.
  • A disqualified person cannot work at a member firm unless the firm obtains relief.
Trigger Rule
Felony conviction Any felony within the past 10 years. Includes a guilty plea and nolo contendere
Disqualifying misdemeanor Within the past 10 years: misdemeanors involving securities or the business of a broker, bank, or insurer, and listed offenses such as theft, forgery, false reports, or perjury
Court order Enjoined from acting as an investment adviser, broker-dealer, or in any securities capacity
SEC or SRO action Expelled or suspended by an SRO, or barred by the SEC
Willful violation Found to have willfully violated the federal securities laws
Willful false filing Willfully making a false or misleading statement of a material fact, or willfully omitting a required material fact, in a filing with the SEC or an SRO
  • The 10-year clock starts on the conviction date, not the date of the offense. After 10 years, that conviction alone no longer disqualifies. The same clock covers felonies and disqualifying misdemeanors.
  • Every felony inside 10 years counts, including non-financial felonies. A misdemeanor DUI by itself does not. A theft misdemeanor can, even with no securities or customer funds involved.
  • Nolo contendere (no contest) is treated the same as a guilty plea.

Relief: FINRA Membership Continuance Application, Form MC-400. The firm must show adequate supervision, that the association is consistent with the public interest, and that conditions or restrictions will be imposed. FINRA may approve with heightened supervision or restricted activities.

Ineligibility for membership or association

  • Under FINRA By-Laws (Article III), ineligibility and statutory disqualification are the same bar. The By-Laws point to statutory disqualification in the Securities Exchange Act of 1934. Ineligibility is not a wider, separate test.
  • A firm must not admit, and must not continue, the association of a person who is statutorily disqualified.
  • Events that bar association: a felony or disqualifying-misdemeanor conviction within 10 years; an SEC or SRO suspension, bar, or expulsion; an injunction from securities activities; a willful securities-law violation; a willfully false or misleading filing of a material fact.
  • The only path to keep that person is Form MC-400, and only if FINRA grants relief. While that application is pending, the person may not associate with the firm or do the work the disqualification blocks. Association waits until FINRA grants relief.
  • A pending indictment is not a statutory disqualification. It is a reportable event and a Form U4 disclosure. Disqualification is based on a conviction, or on a regulatory bar, suspension, or injunction.

If the firm lets an unregistered person do work that requires registration:

  • Fines against the firm.
  • FINRA may suspend the firm's membership.
  • Supervisors can be sanctioned personally, including for negligence.
  • The unregistered person can be sanctioned too. "Associated person" includes anyone performing securities-business functions for a member, registered or not.
  • Firms screen before hire and watch for new disqualifying events. If a registered person becomes disqualified, the firm restricts the person promptly and files for relief if it wants to keep them.

State registration (blue-sky laws)

  • Blue-sky laws are state securities statutes. They sit beside federal and FINRA rules. The name refers to schemes with no more basis than so many feet of blue sky.
  • FINRA registration alone does not authorize business. Register in every state where the person does business or has clients.
  • States may add stricter requirements. They may not set a lesser standard than federal or FINRA rules.
  • States may deny, suspend, or revoke a registration on their own, separate from FINRA.

Exam traps

  • A non-registered person never accepts an order, even an unsolicited one, and never writes the order down to hand off. Transfer the call.
  • Pre-approved marketing materials may be handed out by a non-registered person. Unapproved materials are sales activity.
  • A Series 7 or Series 6 registration does not authorize supervision. Supervision needs a principal exam such as the Series 24.
  • The SIE needs no sponsor and can be taken at 18. Every top-off exam needs a sponsoring firm. Both exams are required before registration is effective.
  • Form U4 is filed by the firm through CRD. There is no self-filing.
  • Form U4 amendments: 30 days after learning the facts; 10 days after a statutory disqualification occurs.
  • Fingerprints are an SEC requirement sent to the FBI. Missing them within 30 days after FINRA receives Form U4 makes the registration inactive.
  • No separate 3-year investigation window. Verify the Form U4 within 30 days of filing. The form itself asks for 10 years of employment and 5 years of residence.
  • A felony charge goes on Form U4. Statutory disqualification waits for a conviction (or a bar, suspension, or injunction). A pending indictment does not by itself disqualify.
  • The 10-year disqualification clock runs from the conviction date. Nolo contendere counts as a conviction. Any felony counts. A DUI misdemeanor does not; a theft misdemeanor can.
  • Ineligibility and statutory disqualification are the same bar. Relief is Form MC-400, filed by the firm.
  • Unregistered activity can sanction the firm, negligent supervisors, and the unregistered person.
  • Series 63, Series 65, and Series 66 are state blue-sky exams. A state may be stricter than FINRA and may not be looser. FINRA registration does not cover state registration.
  • The Regulatory Element is annual, due December 31, not every 3 years. CE inactive blocks all registered work and does not end the registration.
  • The Firm Element needs a written needs analysis and applies to every registered person.

Form U4 "yes" answers that need detail:

  • Criminal: felony charges or convictions, any subject. Misdemeanor charges or convictions that are investment-related, or that involve fraud, false statements, wrongful taking of property, bribery, perjury, forgery, counterfeiting, or extortion. A listed misdemeanor counts with no securities connection.

  • Reason for termination (voluntary, terminated, permitted to resign, and so on).
  • Pending customer complaints, internal reviews or investigations, and regulatory investigations.

  • After termination, the person stays under regulator jurisdiction for at least 2 years.

Misleading information and omissions

  • A misleading statement or a material omission on Form U4 or Form U5 is a serious violation for the individual who supplied it and the firm that filed it.
  • Omitting a required fact is treated the same as stating a false one.

Fine Money penalty on the person and/or the firm
Suspension Temporary removal from the industry
Permanent bar A penalty: a lifetime prohibition imposed in a disciplinary case

  • An SRO bar is one of the events that causes statutory disqualification. A willful misstatement can produce that status; a permanent bar is a separate penalty that may or may not follow.
  • Statutory disqualification is not always permanent. The firm can seek relief with Form MC-400. A bar from a disciplinary action is for life.
  • The individual must tell the truth. The firm must do reasonable due diligence before filing. Checking CRD is part of that. Taking the person's word, and ignoring obvious red flags, can make the firm liable.
  • A Form U5 that says "voluntary resignation" when the firm fired the person for cause is a misleading filing, even if the person asked for that wording.

Customer complaints

  • Keep a written record of every customer complaint. Written means letters, emails, and texts, including a text to a representative's personal phone. The device does not matter.
  • Keep complaint records at least 4 years. Some records are kept 6 years. The lesson does not name which records use the longer period.
  • Form U4 / Form U5 disclosure: a written complaint alleging damages of $5,000 or more. Merit is irrelevant. A complaint the firm thinks is frivolous is still disclosed if the dollar allegation meets $5,000.
  • Report to FINRA within 30 days any written complaint alleging theft, misappropriation of funds or securities, or forgery, at any dollar amount.
  • A complaint under $5,000 stays in the firm's file and in the quarterly statistics. The $5,000 line decides only the Form U4 disclosure.
  • Each quarter, send FINRA a statistical summary of all written complaints, any dollar amount, by the 15th day of the month after the quarter ends.

Dispute resolution. Firms must tell customers about FINRA's process.

  • Arbitration is binding. An arbitrator or panel decides.
  • Mediation is voluntary and non-binding. Neither side must accept the result.
  • A firm cannot make a customer waive the right to complain to a regulator. A pre-dispute arbitration agreement does not block a complaint to FINRA, the SEC, or a state regulator.

Potential red flags

Heightened scrutiny is warranted when a firm sees:

  • Unusually many complaints against one representative.
  • Churning: frequent buying and selling that generates commissions and does not benefit the customer. That is different from legitimate active trading.
  • Heavy concentration in one security or product type.
  • A lifestyle or unexplained wealth that does not match compensation.
  • Refusal to take vacation. Time away lets someone else review the accounts. It is not a sign of dedication.
  • Requests to use personal email, a personal phone, or personal texts for business, which sit outside firm recordkeeping and supervision.
  • Elderly or financially unsophisticated customers placed in complex or high-risk products.

  • Large wires or major account changes soon after the account opens.

  • FINRA expects supervisory systems that catch these signs. If the firm knew, or should have known, and did nothing, the firm shares responsibility. Actual knowledge is not required. Supervisory failures are a common exam finding.

Reportable events

Two 30-day channels. Failure to report is its own violation.

  1. The firm reports listed events to FINRA under the reporting-requirements rule, within 30 calendar days of when the firm knew or should have known.
  2. The firm amends Form U4 within 30 days of learning the facts. An amendment tied to a statutory disqualification is due within 10 days after the disqualification occurs.

The associated person promptly tells the firm. The firm tells FINRA. Each leg is a separate duty. Filings go through the FINRA Gateway. If a listed event about an individual is disclosed on Form U4 in the required format, and the filing says that disclosure also satisfies the reporting rule, a second report of that same event is not required.

Events the firm reports to FINRA:

  • A finding that the firm or an associated person violated securities, insurance, commodities, financial, or investment-related laws, rules, or conduct standards, by a regulator, an SRO, or a business or professional organization.
  • The firm must also report within 30 days when it concludes, or reasonably should have concluded, that it or an associated person committed such a violation.
  • Any written customer complaint alleging theft, misappropriation of funds or securities, or forgery, at any dollar amount.
  • Being named a defendant or respondent in a regulator or SRO proceeding alleging a securities, insurance, or commodities violation.
  • Regulatory discipline: registration denied, expelled, enjoined, cease-and-desist, suspended, barred, or otherwise disciplined by a securities, insurance, or commodities regulator or an SRO.
  • Criminal: indicted, convicted, or a plea of guilty or no contest to any felony, or to a listed misdemeanor (purchase or sale of a security, theft, forgery, bribery, perjury, embezzlement, or misappropriation of funds or securities). The charge starts the clock.
  • Association, as a director, officer, partner, controlling stockholder, sole proprietor, or associated person, with a financial firm that was suspended, expelled, or had its registration denied or revoked, or with a bank, trust company, or other financial institution convicted of or pleading no contest to a felony or misdemeanor.
  • Securities- or commodities-related civil litigation or arbitration, financial-related insurance litigation or arbitration, or a financial-services damages claim by a customer, broker, or dealer, disposed of by judgment, award, or settlement for more than $15,000. The threshold is more than $25,000 when the member firm itself is the party.
  • Becoming statutorily disqualified, or doing business with a statutorily disqualified person who has not been approved for membership or association.
  • The firm's own discipline: it suspends or terminates an associated person, withholds compensation, fines the person more than $2,500, or otherwise significantly limits the person's activities.

A felony conviction within the past 10 years is also a statutory disqualification.

Exam traps

  • The firm files Form U4 and Form U5. Form U5 is due within 30 days of termination. Amend it until final disposition. Jurisdiction lasts at least 2 years after the person leaves.
  • Disclose charges, not only convictions. A listed misdemeanor need not be a securities offense.
  • Only a willful material misstatement or omission on an SEC or SRO filing can create statutory disqualification from that filing. A permanent bar is a different thing: a disciplinary penalty. An SRO bar does make the person statutorily disqualified. Relief from the status is Form MC-400.
  • Calling a for-cause firing a "voluntary resignation" on Form U5 is a misleading filing by the firm.
  • Complaint math: file every written complaint; keep it at least 4 years; disclose on Form U4/Form U5 at $5,000; report theft, misappropriation, or forgery to FINRA within 30 days at any amount; send quarterly stats by the 15th.
  • An arbitration clause does not waive a customer's right to complain to FINRA, the SEC, or a state.
  • Never taking vacation, and using a personal phone or email for business, are red flags. The firm is liable for what it should have known.
  • OBA: prior written notice, including an unpaid board seat. Passive investments are exempt. If the activity is a securities transaction, use the selling-away rule.
  • Selling away: notice always. Compensation (including a gift or an expense reimbursement) also requires written approval and supervision as the firm's own business. No compensation: written acknowledgment, and the firm may set conditions. Immediate family with no selling compensation is excluded.
  • Pay-to-play: $250 per election only if the person can vote for that official. Any other contribution starts a 2-year ban on the firm's business with that entity. Look-back is 2 years. The MSRB writes and collects; FINRA enforces against broker-dealers. Unpaid volunteer time is not a contribution. A fundraiser ticket is.
  • Gifts: $300 per person per year in total. Entertainment counts only when the giver attends, and then it has no fixed dollar cap. Absent-giver tickets are gifts. Older questions may still say $100.
  • Sponsor trips tied to a product sales quota are prohibited. An in-house bonus is allowed only for total production.
  • Reportable-event window is 30 days, not 10 and not 60, except the 10-day Form U4 amendment after a statutory disqualification occurs.
  • Liens, bankruptcies, and unpaid judgments are Form U4 items. They are not criminal, and they are not on the reporting-rule event list. A felony charge is on both channels.
  • Litigation or settlement reporting: over $15,000, or over $25,000 if the member firm is the party. A firm fine above $2,500 is a reportable disciplinary event.

Outside business activities

  • An outside business activity (OBA) is business a registered person does outside the relationship with the member firm.
  • Examples: a board seat, a side business such as real estate or insurance outside the firm, paid speaking, and paid teaching or tutoring.
  • Give the firm prior written notice that describes the activity, before it starts. Starting first is a violation even if the firm would have approved.
  • The firm then looks for a conflict of interest, customer confusion with the firm's business, and any need for extra supervision. The firm may approve (with or without conditions), restrict, or deny.
  • Notice is required. Written approval is not the OBA rule.
  • A board seat needs prior written notice whether or not it is paid.
  • Exempt from the OBA notice rule: passive investments (owning rental property or stocks) and activity that is instead a private securities transaction.

Private securities transactions (selling away)

  • A private securities transaction, also called selling away, is any securities transaction outside the regular course or scope of the person's employment with the member firm.
  • It covers any security: stocks, bonds, private placements, promissory notes, and others.
  • Excluded: transactions among immediate family when the person receives no selling compensation.

Political contributions (pay-to-play)

  • Pay-to-play means political contributions made to obtain municipal securities business.
  • The MSRB writes the rule and collects the reports. The MSRB does not enforce it. FINRA enforces it against broker-dealers. The SEC and federal banking regulators cover bank dealers.
  • Covered people: municipal finance professionals (MFPs) and executive officers of broker-dealers.
  • De minimis: an associated person may contribute up to $250 per election to an official the person is entitled to vote for. A primary and a general election are separate elections, so $250 to each can fit the exception.
  • Over $250, or any contribution (even $1) to an official the person cannot vote for, bans the firm from municipal securities business with that government entity for 2 years from the contribution date.
  • The ban hits the firm even if the MFP acted without the firm's knowledge. There is no "we didn't know" defense.
  • Look-back: contributions in the 2 years before the firm seeks that municipal business are reviewed and can trigger the ban.
  • A contribution includes cash, in-kind help (such as paying a fundraiser's costs), and event tickets. Unpaid personal volunteer time, such as phone banking or staffing an event, is not a contribution.
  • Firms report all political contributions to the MSRB quarterly.
