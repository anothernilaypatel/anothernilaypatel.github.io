One October day, walking across Wentworth's campus, I saw a flyer for a Techstars Startup Weekend that the university was hosting. Wentworth students could attend for free, and even though it meant committing the whole weekend, I signed up on the spot.

After the opening rounds of idea generation and pitching, teams began to form, and I joined a team of eleven building a B2C travel app for people on business trips to unfamiliar cities. The idea was a decentralized map where locals upload their favorite places, so newcomers get realistic, locally curated recommendations instead of generic search results or review sites like TripAdvisor. What made the team unusual was its range: members spanned high school students to PhDs, and backgrounds from an artist to several computer scientists. Some teammates even took a 45-minute tram ride to Faneuil Hall to interview tourists about what they would want from the app.

My part was the technical build. I developed an accurate, geospatially aware map using JavaScript geolocation, and a Python tool that made video montages for the team.

After an intense weekend, we presented our final pitch and placed 2nd. The pitches are no longer on YouTube, but the one recording I could still find is [this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8Kd95VEywaU). Part of the prize was studio space for the season, but with members at such different stages, most of us still finishing degrees, it was hard to keep the project going.

The obvious lesson was that you can build anything with anyone. The more useful one was that in any competition, or any exam, you are optimizing for the audience that makes the decision. Naming the team showed this best. We started as "My Little Adventure," but most of the judges preferred "My Not So Little Adventure," because the original name seemed to limit future growth. I still think the longer name is a mouthful, but I wasn't the audience; the judges were.
