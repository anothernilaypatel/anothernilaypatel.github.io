HackPrinceton came around the same time as exams, but I knew I had to show up for this one since my friends from HackHarvard were flying from across the country to compete in this one. After wrapping up exams, I was ready to compete in a hackathon without asterisks. HackPrinceton had a stronger emphasis on merging finance with computer science, with a ton of hedge funds sponsoring the event and even having their own tracks to compete in. For this hackathon, we decided to specialize in competing in Coinbase's "Best Use of OnChainKit". The reasoning behind this was that a niche category would typically have fewer participants. It wasn't a lie that we placed 2nd, but when there were only 4 participating teams, since the track had a high floor, it no longer seems like it is an accolade, but being able to compete in a hard track was still a journey itself.

With fewer participants comes having more personal conversations with the people judging us. That weekend, we had multiple ideas and multiple tracks to pick from, and the ideas ranged from game-ifying predictive indicators into a poker-like game to compete in the quantitative finance tracks to creating a tool to send money through stablecoins to those who have just faced some natural disaster. We ended up settling for the latter since that was able to utilize the greatest number of tools in creative ways. The concept was to have people and organizations send money through Solidity contracts, also known as smart contracts. This money can only be accessed once people agree to terms created by the giver, in which organizations can put a small Terms of Service behind these payments. This prevents the situation in which people use the funds to rebuild their homes in flood zones, raking in net profits. This is also more convenient since it goes from device to device without needing excess transportation or businesses.

One of my most memorable moments came from part of the hackathon's activities. With hackathons becoming more popular and AI expanding, the number of participants at these events started to get inflated, and it made it harder to predict the outcomes of these events. However, HackPrinceton offered a [schedule of speakers](https://docs.google.com/document/d/111nivsg1wPCAeQJqp7DcvVR-sw8lW3H2g904TjF6mFA/edit?usp=sharing), and I made it my goal to go to all of these events on top of programming. The one that stuck out the most was Batu Arisoy's lecture on optimizing large language models. I ended up taking notes like it was a proper lecture to fully understand how LLMs crunch massive prompts through encodings, embeddings, and tokenization. My phone is littered with notes and screenshots of his blackboards and presentations. I cannot say that I have nearly as much knowledge as Mr. Arisoy, but I was able to witness the concept of what it means to have a career that pays to specialize. He probably has no idea who I am, but he made me walk out with a lasting impact, both for the topic and for my career.
