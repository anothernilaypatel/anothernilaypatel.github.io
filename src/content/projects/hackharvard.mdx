There is no placement listed for HackHarvard because I was never officially part of it. I wasn't a registered competitor, my name isn't on any of the team's material, and none of the team's results are mine to claim. Even so, it turned into one of the most useful weekends I spent at university.

It started when a friend messaged me while I was studying at Wentworth. He was coming to Boston for HackHarvard and wanted to meet up before it began, so on a few hours' notice I walked 45 minutes to Harvard's campus and spent the evening with his team, most of whom were his university friends. When the hackathon kicked off, I decided to stay and help from the sidelines as an informal project manager.

The project was a third-party system that audits and corrects records of doctor-patient visits, using a patient's family history and previous visits as its input. My job was to keep the team moving: allocating work, planning sleep schedules, and keeping everyone fueled. Because I wasn't a competitor, I deliberately stayed out of the team's code repository.

My biggest contribution came at 1 a.m., when two of our backend developers were already asleep. I worked out a design for a seamless file-in, file-out flow, in which each visit record goes into the audit system and a corrected record comes back out, ready to feed into the next visit. Rather than writing it myself, I picked up a whiteboard and a marker and taught the frontend engineer how to build the backend for that piece, so the team could keep making progress overnight.

Judging brought a lesson of its own. The team lost some points because a judge who worked in healthcare was concerned the project could raise HIPAA issues. None of us knew healthcare policy well enough to answer her, and she was right to ask: in a regulated industry, compliance is part of the product rather than an afterthought. Since then, [DocScribe](https://www.docscribe.health/en) has become a real company working on a similar problem, which is a good sign that the idea was worth pursuing.

What I took away from the weekend is that you can add real value to a team without writing a line of code, by breaking the problem down, unblocking people, and teaching.
